# How a job runs

Every studio runs its jobs in the same six steps, in the same order. The steps are the shape; the studio's own pages and Sean's taste fill them in. This guide is the detail behind the table on the lead's page.

## The six steps

### 1. Front door

The idea becomes a clear ask. Follow `craftwork/guides/front-door.md` and the studio's front-door page. Ends when Sean accepts the read-back.

### 2. What good looks like

Find the best real examples of this kind of work, vet each one for how it actually turned out, and measure what makes it good: its structure, its numbers, its choices. Measure, don't admire; vague praise makes generic work, measurements make targets. Start from the studio's shelf of good work and add what this job needs. If Sean brought a reference, it's the main one: ask him what he loves about it, because his answer is the best guess to test.

Make it visible: put the examples in front of Sean with what was measured, and ask "is this what you love about it?" Then rank what makes the best example great. That ranking is what every later step is judged against. Add any example worth keeping to the studio's shelf.

### 3. Lock in order

Settle first the decisions that would cause the most rework if they changed later, one at a time, each with Sean's yes. For a film that's the words, then the sound, then the picture; for software it might be what it must do, then how it fits together, then how it looks. The studio's pages name its order. Never polish anything that sits on top of a decision that isn't locked yet.

### 4. The whole thing, rough

Make every part, cheaply, so Sean can judge the whole shape before anything is polished. Nothing missing, nothing finished. This is the cheap place to move fast and to change course.

### 5. Craft and fixes

Make it good. Name the three or four moments that carry the piece and protect them; it's easy to slide back to safe. When Sean gives notes, give each one the smallest fix that answers it, and don't rework what he didn't mention. Before he sees each new version, compare it against the best example from step 2 and fix the biggest gaps first.

### 6. Finish

Deliver it, with proof Sean can see: the real thing, rendered or run, plus any measure the studio uses for finished work. Then the short scorecard (`craftwork/guides/review-pages.md`). The job is done when Sean approves it.

## Checkpoints

Each step ends at a checkpoint where Sean decides one thing, on a house review page. Between checkpoints, keep going without asking. Dates are pace, not checkpoints.

Sean's checkpoints are the evaluation. His pick, his notes and his scorecard are how a studio learns whether it's good, not an agent grading its own steps.

When Sean is in a hurry, compress the steps; never skip one or change their order. Step 2 is what saves review rounds later, so keep at least its ranked "what makes it great".

## Helpers

- **Breadth, at once.** When the work needs several kinds of expertise, send helpers in parallel, each with a clear piece, the studio's expertise brief for its area, and what to hand back. Helpers add to the work; they never send it back or sign it off. The lead puts it together.
- **The fresh check.** For facts, numbers and tests, a helper who didn't make the thing reads it cold and reports what's wrong. Use it before Sean sees a version that makes claims or has tests. It checks truth, not taste; taste is Sean's. Now and then a review page shows Sean one of the fresh check's calls, so he can spot-check it.
- **Codex helpers in the background** hang forever waiting for more input unless the launch closes it. Add `< /dev/null` to any background `codex exec`, or pass no input stream from a script.

## Records

Keep two, plainly written, in the job's folder:

- **Decisions**: each of Sean's decisions with his reason in his words, as they happen.
- **Where it stands**, at every stop: what exists, how it was checked, what isn't done, and the next action. The next session starts by reading it.

Nothing else. No logs of agent steps, no self-scores.

## Between studios

Borrow expertise from any studio freely: read its briefs and guides, or send a helper with them. Work passes from one studio to another only through Sean, at the second studio's front door.
