# Layout and hierarchy

This brief is about arranging a screen or a page so the eye reads it in the right order. Read it when a job first lays out a screen, when something looks off and nobody can say why, and before the spacing is settled. Type has its own brief. How scroll drives motion is in the spectacle tool guide, and finished films belong to Motioncraft.

## Start from the content, not the frame

A beginner starts with the frame: the top bar, the sidebar, where the logo goes. A master starts with one real thing the product must do, and designs only that. The frame exists to serve the features, so it waits until a few exist. Sometimes the feature becomes the whole page. The UK visa checker's best screen is one question, three answers and one green button, with nothing else to look at.

Work with the real words and the hardest case: the longest name, the empty list, the error. Work in greyscale first. An order that holds up in grey will survive the colour that comes later. Colour added early makes a muddled layout look finished. Motioncraft's look brief says the same about films: value first, then colour.

Then ask what the visitor came to do, because it pulls the layout three ways:

- **To get a job done.** Predictable places, steady density, standard controls. The design lends a voice; it never moves the furniture.
- **To read and understand.** A calm column at a comfortable width, with clear wayfinding. The look owns the frame around the column, not the column.
- **To be persuaded, or to have an experience.** The look may own the whole composition, as long as the page still works: the offer is clear, the action is visible, and the text can be read.

## Hierarchy: what the eye reads first

Hierarchy is the order of importance the eye sees. Five things set it: size, weight, colour and contrast, space, and position. A sixth is breaking a pattern once: the one item that differs in colour, weight or size gets looked at first. That trick works once per screen; used three times, the breaks cancel out.

**Size is the bluntest lever.** Asked to do everything, it gives a giant title and unreadable small print. Weight and a softer colour do more for less. Three or four text colours by importance carry most screens. Linear uses four greys, from near-white to dim, and one indigo.

**To lift something, turn the rest down.** Hierarchy works like a mixing desk: most fixes lower a fader rather than raise one. If the thing that matters won't stand out, quieten what sits around it. If the numbers that matter are lost, look for what took the strongest colour, and move that colour to them.

**Labels are often not needed.** A date, a sum of money or a web address explains itself. A label can fold into its value ("Shipped Tuesday"), or sit small and soft beside a large value. Only where people hunt for the label itself, as on a nutrition panel, should the label lead.

**Size a heading for its job, not its rank in the code.** In an app, the page title often just names what is below it, so it can be small. The page's code can still call it the main heading.

**Rank the buttons before styling them.** Usually one action matters most on a screen, one or two matter some, and the rest are rarely used. The main one gets a solid, high-contrast button. Secondary ones get an outline. Rare ones look like links. A delete button stays quiet until the confirmation step, where it really is the main action.

The check is the squint test. Blur your eyes, or the screenshot. Can you still name the first thing, the second thing, and the main groups, in order?

## Space carries meaning

**The gap inside a group must be smaller than the gap between groups.** That is how the eye tells what belongs together. A heading sits closer to the text it heads than to the text above it; twice the space above as below is a good start. A form label sits close to its own field. A label floating halfway between two fields gets the wrong data typed in.

**Begin with more space than seems right, then remove it.** Space added a little at a time stops at barely enough. A part that looks loose on its own usually looks right among the rest of the screen. Dense layouts are fine, but density should be chosen, not fallen into.

**Pick spacing from a short list.** A spacing scale is a few set values whose steps grow as they get bigger: small steps at the small end, where a few pixels show, and big steps at the large end, where they don't. With a scale, choosing becomes comparing a guess with its neighbours. GOV.UK's runs in steps of five; Linear's rows are 28 pixels tall with 2-pixel gaps. Whether the base unit is four or eight matters less than having the list.

**Separate with space before boxes.** Try space first, then a slight change of background, then a soft shadow. Use a border last. Borders round everything make a page busy, and a box inside a box is almost always wrong.

## Width, grids and alignment

**Size each thing to its content, not to the screen.** Running text stops at a comfortable line length even inside a wide layout. A narrow form in a wide page looks lost; the fix is columns, not stretching. A field's width hints at the length of the answer, so a stretched field misleads.

**Fix what shouldn't stretch.** A sidebar that takes a share of the screen balloons on a wide one and crowds a narrow one. Give it a set width for what it holds, and let the main area take the rest. Linear's sidebar is 232 pixels.

**Alignment is what makes a grid felt.** A grid is something to use inside parts of a page, not a rule over all of it. Pick two or three lines that things start from and reuse them everywhere. Six different starting lines means nothing was decided. Align text to the letters themselves, their baselines and the tops of their capitals, not to the boxes around them.

**Symmetry or not.** A centred layout is calm and obvious. An off-centre one has more energy, but every part hanging off to one side needs something to hold it: another block, a strong alignment, a grid line. Centring everything is not a decision. Centring one moment on purpose is.

**Where the skills disagree: the frame.** The minimalist skill and GOV.UK keep pages in a narrow container; GOV.UK stops growing at 960 pixels. The frontend-taste skill's study of award sites found strong pages often run to the edges, and the centred container showed up mostly on sites judged stock. Both cap the width of running text. They differ on the frame, and that turns on whether the page has large things to fill the width: pictures, display type, fields of colour. A tool or a form wants the container. A spectacle may not.

## Density

Density is how much sits in each part of the screen. Match it to how often the screen is used and how hard its decisions are. Linear is dense and calm at once, because a few strict choices repeat everywhere: one typeface, four greys, one accent, rows of one height. Things 3 is calm the other way, by leaving most of the screen white. The high-end and minimalist skills both ask for huge padding between sections; that describes a look, not a law.

## Big pages and long scrolling stories

Sean loves scroll effects and reveals: "Scrolling is a great way to tell the story." A long page is a sequence of screens. Each screen needs its own composition, and the sequence needs a rhythm.

- **One thing per screen.** One statement, one object, one project. Each section carries one claim, or it goes.
- **Vary the arrangement, carry the idea.** Alternate dense and empty, picture and type, light and dark grounds. No two neighbours share a layout. A repeated module belongs only to a true archive: Lando Norris's helmet grid is an archive, so repetition is right there.
- **Spend the showpieces in a few places.** Lando's site puts its spectacle in two places, the 3D head and the helmets. The rest is a tidy magazine layout.
- **Keep the skeleton, change the surface.** Shopify's Renaissance edition keeps nearly the same chapters and the same card in every edition, then dresses everything the eye lands on as a museum. That is how a brand gets turned on its head without getting lost.
- **Let the reader's hand set the pace, and hold the rest still.** On Segerman's site the wheel moves one column and the three columns never change places. The Mechanical Watch article puts a plain sentence of setup before each model and lets the reader move it.
- **Keep the ask in one place.** Scout Motors runs a 20-screen film, and Reserve never leaves the top bar, in the one orange kept for things you can press.
- **Say what it is, and offer the action, on the first screen.** Break the system once, after it is set, so the rule shows. End on a designed scene, not a block of link columns.
- **Design the phone as its own page.** Lando's four-column grids become two. Segerman's one-screen layout becomes an ordinary scrolling page.

## How it goes wrong

- **Everything shouting.** Every number large, every button solid, every row the same weight. The eye has nowhere to land. Turn most of it down.
- **Even spacing that groups nothing.** Same gap above and below every heading, label and list item. Rank the gaps.
- **Filling the width.** Paragraphs across the whole screen, fields stretched to match the menu bar. Give content its own width; use columns for the leftover space.
- **Centred everything.** A centred stack of heading, paragraph and two buttons, screen after screen. Anchor to the left and centre one moment.
- **A strong first screen, then stock blocks.** Three icon cards, an accordion, a footer of links. The idea has to reach the last screen.
- **A device copied without a job.** The frontend-taste skill and the impeccable skill ban tiny labels above headings and numbered sections ("01, 02, 03"). The taste skill's own study found numbered sections more often on top sites than on weak ones, though tiny labels were commoner on the weak ones. The house style uses small capital labels, and Poor Charlie's Almanack uses huge numbers as its menu, because there the numbers are the chapters. The test is whether the device carries information.

## What a strong designer would notice

- Squinting at it, they can't tell what comes first.
- A heading floating halfway between two blocks.
- Six left edges where two would do.
- A sidebar or field stretched because the screen was wide.
- A strong colour spent on chrome while the data sits in grey.
- Two buttons of equal weight when one action matters more.
- Boxes inside boxes, borders on everything.
- The same layout three sections in a row.
- A phone layout that is the desktop squeezed.
- The ask moving about, or vanishing, on a long page.
