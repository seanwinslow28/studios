# Presentations

Read this at the start of any deck or talk, before a single slide is drawn, and again before it is rehearsed. A strong presentation is clear and a spectacle at once. Clear means the story works with the screen switched off. Spectacle, in Sean's sense, means a brand's design system turned on its head: interactive, inventive, a little weird. This brief covers the story skeleton first, because the look is built around it, and then the look.

## Why both, and why in that order

Sean's practice deck is the lesson. It had good bones and almost no visuals, because nobody asked what good looks like before it was made. More polish on the slides would not have fixed that. Looking at real examples of the talk it should be, then designing to that bar, would have.

The shelf shows the opposite failure too. Shopify's Renaissance edition is a gorgeous spectacle wrapped round a catalogue: the last chapter does not answer the first, and most of its 187 headlines are feature names. Spotify Wrapped 2024 kept the structure and turned up the colour, and listeners still felt short-changed. A tidy deck with good bones is not a spectacle. A spectacle with no bones is a screensaver. The work needs both, and the bones come first because every visual choice hangs on them.

## The story skeleton

### Pick the shape for the room

For a one-to-one, lead with the answer. Set out the situation, what went wrong, and the question it raises, then give the answer, in that order and quickly. The person across the table wants to judge the answer, not wait for it.

For a big room, tell it as a story. A story here means conflict and contrast: what people expected against what is true, before against after. Open inside the problem, not with thanks, a biography or an agenda. Motioncraft's brief on story says the same for film: start later than feels safe, and cut from the front until the first beat holds a problem.

Either way, the point and why it matters to this audience fit in one sentence, or there is no talk yet.

### Every headline states its point

Each slide's headline is a claim, not a label. "Checkout" is a label. "Half of buyers quit at the payment step" is a claim. Read the headlines alone, in order, and they should tell the whole story.

This pulls against the quiet, photographic school of slide design, which wants as few words on screen as possible and the point said aloud. Both are right about something. Words on screen compete with the speaker's voice, and a slide is read in a glance or not at all. So on a big-room slide, the claim can be short and set large over a picture, or held in the speaker's mouth while the screen shows the evidence, as Hans Rosling and Bret Victor do. The claim still exists, written down, for every slide. A deck whose headlines can't be read as a story has no skeleton, however beautiful it is.

### Plant something early that the close pays off

Put something in the opening minutes that the last minutes return to, changed. Bret Victor builds a three-line list at the start of his talk, and its last line comes back alone near the end as the question he leaves the room with. Ciechanowski's watch article shows the finished mechanism first, and every chapter earns one more part of it. Rosling plants a quiz and pays it off, but a quarter of the way in, so his close has to lean on a separate request. The best payoff is often the first image or object coming back with a new meaning.

### One idea per slide, and the reveal happens in silence

One slide, one idea. If it needs two, it is two slides. A busy slide becomes a short run of clean ones sharing one treatment.

The reveal follows a rhythm: say the setup, click, pause, then name what they see. The pause is where the audience works it out for themselves, and that is the moment they remember. Victor's best moment is 18 seconds of near silence while his finished animation plays, then one sentence naming it. Ciechanowski's version puts the reader's own hand on the click: the text says what to try, the reader tries it, and only then does the text name it.

Rosling is the useful exception. He explains his axes in full and asks one question before anything moves, then talks through the motion like a sports commentator. When the reveal is data running through time, narration can carry it. Silence is the default; breaking it is a choice made on purpose. Spotify's 2025 fix adds a third way: ask the audience to guess before the reveal, so they have something at stake when it lands.

### A cold critic reviews it before anyone sees it

Before the deck goes out, someone who did not make it reads it cold, against the audience's own rules: what this room values, how it decides, the words it uses, what it will push back on. This is not a taste review. A deck can be lovely and still break the one rule its audience cares about most. The maker can't see that, because they already know what every slide means.

### A rehearsal plan

Rehearsal is planned, not hoped for. Memorise three moments word for word: the opening, the turn and the close. Everything between is known well enough to say naturally, never read. Mark in the notes where each pause goes, so the silent reveals actually stay silent under nerves. Every clip, live demo or interactive scene gets a tested fallback, such as a still, a local copy, or a recording of the demo working, and the whole deck runs offline. The test is simple: could the talk still be given with the screen dark? Finish a little before the time runs out, not on it.

## Then the look: a spectacle built on the skeleton

### Ask what good looks like first

Before any slide is designed, find and show real examples of the kind of spectacle this talk should be. Shopify's Renaissance edition is the starting point when Sean says "you choose". This step is exactly what the practice deck skipped.

### Turn the brand on its head, and keep the skeleton

Shopify's editions show where the line sits. The skeleton stays fixed and familiar between editions: the chapter names, the card format, a plain sans for body text, and the product's own type wherever the product itself appears. Everything the eye lands on changes: oil paintings, script capitals, cream paper. That is the move to copy. Bend the surface hard, keep the structure the audience relies on, and keep the brand recognisable in one or two fixed places so the bend reads as deliberate.

### Make the thing itself the slide

The strongest visuals on the shelf are not pictures of the idea; they are the idea, running. More than half of Victor's talk is five live demos. Five of Rosling's seven sequences are live software he drives. A deck built in code can do this: the interface runs inside the slide, and the audience sees the claim proved in front of them.

### Spend the show where the story turns

A talk has room for three or four showpieces, the same ration Motioncraft uses for a short film: usually the opening, the problem at its worst, the turn and the payoff. Everything else gets craft, not fireworks. Shopify spends almost all its spectacle in the first chapter, which holds all 12 of the page's videos, and the last chapter is close to a plain list. Wrapped 2024 repeated one frame on most cards, and by the fourth it no longer read as spectacle at all.

### Weird needs a plain part beside it

Bruno Simon's site is a driving game, and it still works because a plain menu and reset buttons sit beside the toy. In a deck, the plain part is the headline, the one fixed place for the brand, and slides that rest between the showpieces. Strangeness lands against calm.

### Picture over text, and numbers made visible

A full-screen picture of the real thing beats a description of it. Type sits in the picture's empty space, large, with a colour taken from the picture. Charts are flat, with one highlighted value and the finding said in words. Every big number gets a comparison and a picturable size, and is shown huge.

### Motion between slides carries logic

A change between slides should say "and also", "but" or "here is the proof", not just move. Keep a quiet change for small steps and a bigger move for a new act, and choose one family of transitions for the whole talk. Motioncraft's brief on editing and transitions holds here: a transition with no idea in it is noise. A black screen is a transition too, for when the speaker should have the room's full attention.

## How it goes wrong

- **Good bones, no visuals.** Fix: show examples of what good looks like before designing.
- **A spectacle round a list.** Fix: write the run of headlines first, and check it tells a story.
- **Labels for headlines.** Fix: rewrite each as the claim it makes.
- **An agenda, thanks or an apology as the opening.** Fix: open inside the problem.
- **A payoff with nothing planted.** Fix: choose the close, then plant its seed in the first minutes.
- **Talking over the reveal.** Fix: setup, click, pause, name, marked in the notes.
- **Show everywhere.** Fix: three or four showpieces, quiet in between.
- **The brand bent until it is gone.** Fix: keep the skeleton and one or two fixed brand marks.
- **A demo with no fallback.** Fix: a tested still or recording for every live part.
- **Reviewed only by its maker.** Fix: a cold critic, against the audience's rules.

## What a strong designer would notice

- Headlines that are nouns.
- An opening that could belong to any talk.
- A close that does not return to anything.
- Two ideas on one slide, or a paragraph on screen.
- A reveal the speaker talks over.
- The same layout on every slide by the fourth.
- Every showpiece in the first five minutes.
- A brand so bent it can't be recognised, or not bent at all.
- A big number with nothing to compare it to.
- Transitions that move but don't mean anything.
- A clip with no plan for when it fails.
