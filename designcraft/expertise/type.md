# Type

This brief is about choosing typefaces and setting them on screens and web pages. Read it when a job picks its type, sets its sizes, or when text looks amateur and nobody can say why. Getting the fonts into a page, their files and their licences, is in the type tool guide. Type that moves in a finished film belongs to Motioncraft.

## Choosing a face

**Start from what readers will do.** If they will read at length or work in a tool every day, start with the text face: many weights, a true italic, and tall lowercase letters with open spacing, which stay clear when small. If the job is voice first, such as a brand page, a poster or a spectacle, start with the display face and give it a quiet partner.

**No face means one thing in advance;** what it says depends on the job. Rough leanings hold. A serif reads classic. A rounded sans reads friendly. A plain sans steps back and lets colour, pictures or words carry the personality. Light weights read refined, heavy ones loud.

**Signs of a well-made family:** many weights, a drawn italic, real small capitals, and optical sizes, which are versions redrawn for small or large use. It must also hold every character the real copy needs, or accented names fall back mid-word.

**A working tool can use the system's own font; a branded page wants its own.** One skill's study of award sites found a characterless default face among the commonest faults of weak pages.

**Where the skills disagree: banned faces.** The high-end and minimalist skills ban Inter, Roboto and Helvetica. Yet Linear, the calm and dense end of the shelf, runs on Inter alone, in in-between weights such as 510 and 590. GOV.UK runs on its own face in two weights. The ban is aimed at a face picked without deciding anything. A common face, chosen and tuned on purpose, is a decision.

## Pairing

**One family is the safest way to look coherent.** About a third of the sites in one skill's study used just one. With two, give each a job and never swap them.

**Pair on one clear difference and one shared trait.** The difference might be serif against sans, or narrow against wide. The shared trait might be the height of the lowercase, the shape of the round letters, or the same designer. If nothing comes to mind that they share, try another pair. Match them by eye on lowercase height, not by the size number.

**Where the skills disagree: two faces in one headline.** The frontend-taste skill says never mix them. Lando Norris's site does it on purpose: a plain sans carries nearly everything, and a sharp display face with thick and thin strokes sets single words inside headlines. It won Site of the Year. The skill warns against a habit of generated pages; Lando holds the move across the whole site, so it reads as the brand.

Shopify's Renaissance edition runs four faces, each with one job: a serif for headlines, a script for oversized first letters, a plain sans for the card text, and the product's own face wherever the product appears.

## A type scale

A type scale is a short list of sizes chosen once. Every size in the design comes from it. Neighbouring sizes must look clearly different. Nine sizes that nearly match read as mush; if two sizes look almost the same, make them the same.

**How many sizes is where the sources disagree, and the surface decides.**

- **A product screen** wants a hand-picked list, tight at the small end and wider at the top. Linear's text sits on four small steps, 12, 13, 14 and 16 pixels, and leans on weight and grey to tell roles apart.
- **A long document** suits sizes made by multiplying by a gentle ratio. A steep ratio leaps past the sizes you need.
- **A brand or spectacle page** wants two or three sizes with nothing between: one very large, one for text, one small. The frontend-taste skill's study found the headline at about four to five times the body size on its top sites. Poor Charlie's Almanack sets titles at about eight times its text.

**Size by role, not by tag.** A page title in an app can be small; the code can still call it the main heading.

**On a phone, big things shrink faster than small ones.** Keep the body size steady and bring the headlines down. Poor Charlie's text stays at 18 pixels on a phone while its titles drop from about 140 to about 82. A desktop ratio kept on a phone gives a headline that wraps into a column.

## Measure and leading

Measure is the length of a line; leading is the space between lines.

**Keep reading lines between about 45 and 75 characters.** Longer, and the eye loses its place on the way back. Cap the width of paragraphs even inside a wide layout. Sources differ at the edges, from 35 up to a strict 65 to 75, but all call past about 90 too long.

**Leading follows measure and size.** Longer lines and smaller text want more; bigger text wants less. Body text on screen sits around 1.4 to 1.6 times the type size. Big headlines go much tighter. On the shelf, Lando sets them at 0.82, Poor Charlie's at 0.84, Scout Motors at 0.9 and Segerman at 0.8. Below 1, check that the tails of one line don't hit the next.

**A heading's own lines sit closer than the gap after it.** Otherwise a two-line heading falls apart into the text below.

**Light text on a dark ground** looks bolder, and its letters seem to crowd. Give it a touch more letter spacing and leading. Judge the weight by eye: a regular weight often reads heavy, though a very thin face may need a step up.

**Left-align anything over a few lines.** Centre only short, standalone blocks; if one centred block runs long, cut its words rather than changing the alignment. Justified text needs hyphenation, or gaps open up. The frontend-taste skill says never justify; the books allow it with hyphens on print-like pages.

**Break headlines by phrase,** and check every break at phone width.

## Weight, case and spacing

**Two weights usually carry an interface,** as on GOV.UK: a regular and one heavier. Very thin weights only work large. To make text recede, use a softer colour, not a thin weight.

**Big type doesn't need to be bold.** In one skill's study, about half the headlines measured were a regular weight. Size and spacing make type confident.

**Tighten spacing as type grows.** Large type shows gaps that are invisible at reading size; display sizes usually want slightly negative letter spacing. Fix the bad pairs by eye: a T, W, V or Y beside a round or short letter leaves a hole. Kerning is the space between one pair of letters; tracking is the spacing of a whole line.

**Open up capitals a little, never lowercase.** Capitals are all one height, so at default spacing they look cramped. Lowercase and italic letters are drawn to sit close and fall apart when spaced out. Scattering tiny spaced-out capitals around a page is a tell: the frontend-taste skill found it on 16% of its top sites and 57% of its weak ones.

**Use the family's real styles.** No slanted upright letters passed off as italic, no shrunken capitals passed off as small caps, no letters pulled wider or narrower to fit. Use a drawn narrow width instead. Use the optical size made for the size you set; a display version used small goes frail.

## Display type that carries a brand

Display type is the voice; body text is the medium, plain and easy to read.

- Poor Charlie's Almanack sets its titles in a light serif at about 140 pixels over a book serif at 18. The jump in scale does the work that motion does elsewhere.
- Scout Motors sets headlines in the wide cut of its own family, at 72 pixels with tight leading.
- Segerman uses a heavy all-capitals face for the one big headline, and a plain sans in one weight for everything else.

**Keep display lines short:** about five words, two lines at most. A whole sentence at display size is a paragraph that shouts. An active verb and a concrete noun do more than any typeface.

**Pull big round letters left.** At display size an O, C or A at the start of a line looks indented, because its edge curves away. Nudge the line left until it looks aligned with the text below.

## Type as image in a spectacle

At the far end, words stop being read as text and become pictures. Scout sets a few words at 270 pixels. Big letters can be the layout itself: their edges and gaps form the grid, and the small text fits into the spaces they leave. Variable fonts, which hold a range of weights or widths in one file, let type answer the reader live, swelling with the pointer or the scroll. That live behaviour is Designcraft's; type that plays the same every time is Motioncraft's.

**Keep real text in the page.** Igloo Inc draws every letter inside its 3D scene. It won Site of the Year, and scored low on accessibility: nothing can be selected, and a screen reader has nothing to read. Draw type as a picture if the idea needs it, and keep the words as text too.

**Turning a brand on its head still keeps the reading type.** Shopify's Renaissance edition swaps the brand's look for a museum's serif and script. A plain sans still carries the card text, and the product keeps its own type.

## Numbers in tables

- **Use tabular figures in columns.** These are digits drawn to one width, so they stack. Right-align them so the places line up. Use lining figures, the height of capitals, among capitals. Oldstyle figures, which rise and fall like lowercase, suit prose in a serif. Check which the face actually has.
- **Let alignment hold the columns.** Soften the column headings, add row spacing, and drop the grid lines.
- **Stack related details in one cell,** the second line smaller and softer: a name with the role beneath it.
- **Write numbers for people.** "Due Friday" saves the reader counting days. A big value with a small, soft label reads faster than a label and a value at equal weight.

## How it goes wrong

- **Too many sizes,** all close together.
- **Hierarchy by size alone:** a giant title and grey print too small to read.
- **Lines across the whole screen,** and one leading for every size.
- **A default face on a page that should have a voice,** or a fashionable serif picked as a shortcut to looking premium.
- **Typewriter-style faces as a costume** for "technical", rather than for code, data or measurement.
- **Body text set in a face drawn only for display sizes.**
- **Text that jumps as the web font arrives.** The type tool guide shows the fix.

## What a strong designer would notice

- Two sizes that nearly match.
- A paragraph running past 75 characters.
- A big headline with loose leading, or tight leading on body text.
- A gap after a capital T in the headline.
- Spaced-out lowercase, or capitals at default spacing.
- Slanted letters that aren't a real italic.
- Figures in a column that don't line up.
- A headline that breaks badly on a phone.
- Words drawn as pictures with no text behind them.
