# Bret Victor's "Inventing on Principle" (2012)

## What it is

A 54-minute talk by Bret Victor, a designer who builds tools for making things. He gave it on January 20, 2012, at a software conference for students. The date is on his title slide.

His argument has two layers. First: people who make things need to see the effect of every change straight away. He calls this an "immediate connection". Second: you can build a working life around a belief like that, instead of around a skill.

He does not describe the first layer. He shows it, five times, with tools he built and runs live on stage.

See it: https://vimeo.com/906418692

That is his own upload. Timings below are from a viewer's copy of the same recording on YouTube, so they may be a few seconds off.

**Best moment: 33:31 to 33:49.** He has just made a short animation on an iPad by moving a leaf and a rabbit with his fingers, like playing an instrument. He says he will bring it up full screen, then "this is the piece". The finished animation plays for 18 seconds and he says nothing. Only afterwards does he name it: he made that in two minutes. Runner-up: **2:30 to 5:00**, the first demo. Code sits on the right, a picture of a tree on the left. He drags a number in the code and the tree changes while he drags.

## How it turned out

- **Makers of well-known tools credited it in writing.**
  - John Resig, announcing Khan Academy's computer science lessons in August 2012, credited Bret Victor's talk and called it "mind-blowing". He does not give the title.
  - Chris Granger, announcing the Light Table code editor in April 2012, named the talk and two of its demos.
  - Chris Lattner writes on his own site that the Playgrounds feature of Apple's Swift language was "heavily influenced" by Bret Victor's ideas. He names the ideas, not this talk.
- **It keeps coming back.** On Hacker News, a tech news site where readers vote stories up, it reached 233 points in February 2012. It was posted again in 2016 and 2018. Two projects built in answer to it also reached the front page in 2012.
- **Audience, partly unknown.** The largest YouTube copy has 339,934 views and 8,530 likes, read on November 16, 2026. A viewer uploaded that copy. I could not read the count on his own Vimeo upload, so the true total is higher and unknown.
- **No award found.** The evidence is what other makers built afterwards.

## What makes it good, measured

I measured these from a YouTube copy: speaking pace from the automatic captions, visuals from one frame every 10 seconds, and loudness from the sound track.

- **Length and pace:** 54 minutes 20 seconds, about 9,050 words, 167 words a minute.
- **Two halves.** The first 34 minutes are the belief and five demos. The last 20 minutes are why he holds it, told almost without visuals.
- **Five live demos, back to back, 29 minutes in all.** That is 54% of the talk.
  - Code that draws a picture, 2:30 to 9:35.
  - A small platform game, where he rewinds time and sees the character's future path, 10:40 to 16:45.
  - A search routine shown working step by step, 16:50 to 23:15.
  - An electronic circuit that shows its own signals, 23:20 to 28:15.
  - The animation, 29:20 to 34:00.
- **About 20 ordinary slides in 54 minutes.** None has more than about six words. Most are a photo and a name, or a single word such as "Motivation". The slides are dark grey with thin white type.
- **One sentence, repeated.** He states the belief at 1:58. The phrase "immediate connection" comes back 14 times. Four of the five demos end by saying it again.
- **The silences are real.** There are 21 gaps in his speech longer than 4 seconds. The longest are 18 and 19 seconds. My read, from the sound track: the 18-second gap at 33:31 is close to true silence, with no clapping under it. Some of the other gaps are the audience clapping.
- **What the audience can touch:** nothing. But everything on screen answers to his hands as he talks, which is the point he is making.
- **Colour values:** not measured. The copies I could reach are low resolution.

Against Sean's four story rules:

- **Every headline states its point: broken.** The slides carry labels, not claims. The spoken sentence does the headline's job.
- **Plant something early that the close pays off: kept.** At 0:47 a three-line list builds on screen: his principle, Larry's principle, your principle. The third line returns alone at 47:38, and his last sentence asks the audience what they believe in. A grid of the five demos shown at 35:17 also returns at 52:53.
- **One idea per slide: kept,** and one idea per demo.
- **The reveal happens in silence: kept.** Setup, action, pause, then the name for what they saw. The best moment above is the clearest case.

### Why it's here

It shows what to do when the thing itself can be on screen: skip the slides about it and run it. My read: the lesson for Designcraft is one claim, said plainly, then proved five different ways, with the claim repeated after each. The caution is the second half. Twenty minutes with almost nothing to look at reads as a lecture, and every project that credits the talk credits the demos.

## What Sean said about it

Seen on November 17, 2026, with the rest of the shelf. He said nothing about this one by itself.

Of the whole shelf: "all of the options you provided are amazing in their own way and I wouldn't want to throw those out the window." He picked no favourite. It stays on the shelf as one end of the range.
