# Hans Rosling's "The best stats you've ever seen" (TED, 2006)

## What it is

A talk by Hans Rosling, a Swedish professor of global health, given at the TED conference in Monterey, California, in February 2006. It runs just under 20 minutes.

His argument: the old picture of the world as a rich "West" and a poor "third world" is out of date. He proves it with charts that move. Each country is a bubble. He presses play, the years run, and the bubbles travel across the chart. The software, called Trendalyzer, came from Gapminder, a non-profit he started in 2005 with Ola Rosling and Anna Rosling Rönnlund.

See it: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hVimVzgtD6w

Timings below are from this upload on TED's own YouTube channel. It opens with a 24-second TED intro, so the talk itself starts at 0:24.

**Best moment: 4:12 to 5:14.** The first run of the chart. He has spent a minute and a half explaining the two axes and asking one question: are his students right that the world still splits in two? Then he presses play. Forty-one years of data run in 48 seconds while he calls it like a sports commentator. The two groups of bubbles merge into one, he names the result in four words ("a completely new world"), and the room claps for 12 seconds.

## How it turned out

- **Audience.** 16,139,672 plays on TED's site. On TED's YouTube channel, 4,417,823 views and 43,988 likes. All read on November 16, 2026.
- **The chart software was bought.** Gapminder's own history page says Google bought the bubble chart software in 2007 and the team moved to Google's head office in California.
- **He kept the approach.** TED's speaker page lists 10 talks by him between 2006 and 2014, this one first.
- **It grew into a book.** Gapminder says the book that followed, Factfulness (2018), has sold over 2.5 million copies in 45 languages. That is the maker's own figure.
- **No award found.** I looked for one and did not find it. The evidence here is the audience and what happened next.

## What makes it good, measured

I measured these from the YouTube upload. Speaking pace comes from the automatic captions. Visuals come from one frame grabbed every 5 seconds (235 frames of the talk), which I looked through by eye.

- **Length and pace:** 19 minutes 29 seconds of talk, about 3,200 words, 164 words a minute.
- **Seven visual sequences, about 2 minutes 34 seconds each on average.** They run from 35 seconds to 6 minutes 20:
  - A quiz slide, 1:05 to 2:30.
  - Bubble chart of family size against length of life, 2:40 to 6:17.
  - A curve of world income that changes shape over time, 6:17 to 8:40.
  - Bubble chart of money against child survival, 8:40 to 15:00.
  - A cartoon of public data stuck underground, 15:15 to 17:35.
  - Income in China against the United States, 17:45 to 18:20.
  - Internet users against income, 18:30 to 19:50.
- **Five of the seven are live software he drives himself.** Only the quiz and the cartoon are ordinary slides. I saw one text-only slide in the whole talk, on screen for about 15 seconds.
- **How much the visuals carry:** the screen is in shot in 169 of the 235 frames, about 7 in every 10. That is the video editor's choice, not what the room saw, but it shows where the editor thought the talk was.
- **Words on screen:** almost none. Axis labels, the year as a huge pale number behind the bubbles, and a country name when he points at one. The quiz slide has about 30 words.
- **The setup is slow and the run is fast.** For the first chart: 92 seconds of setup, 48 seconds of motion, 12 seconds of applause. During the run he speaks at 204 words a minute, his fastest. His slowest stretch is 132 words a minute, at 12:00 to 13:10, while he picks countries and switches on trails behind them.
- **He is never silent.** There are only four gaps in his speech longer than 3.5 seconds, and the long ones are the audience reacting.
- **Colour and type:** not measured. The only public copies are low resolution. By eye: pale blue chart ground in the first tool, dark navy in the second, one bubble colour per region of the world.

Against Sean's four story rules:

- **Every headline states its point: broken.** There are no headlines. The axes pose a question and he says the point out loud, for example that the gap between rich and poor is a myth (6:36).
- **Plant something early that the close pays off: half kept.** He plants the quiz at 1:05, where students scored worse than chimpanzees would by guessing. He plants the students' "we and them" picture at 3:09. Both pay off at 5:00, a quarter of the way in, not at the close. The close pays off a later request, to free public data.
- **One idea per slide: kept.** Each chart run answers one question.
- **The reveal happens in silence: broken on purpose.** He does say the setup first, in full. Then he talks through the motion at his fastest pace. The silence belongs to the audience.

### Why it's here

It is the proof that a data argument can be the show. My read: the lesson for Designcraft is the order. Explain the axes and ask one question before anything moves, then let time be the motion. It also sits against one of Sean's rules. Rosling narrates his reveals and it works, so the silent reveal is a choice to make, not a law.

## What Sean said about it

Seen on November 17, 2026, with the rest of the shelf. He said nothing about this one by itself.

Of the whole shelf: "all of the options you provided are amazing in their own way and I wouldn't want to throw those out the window." He picked no favourite. It stays on the shelf as one end of the range.
