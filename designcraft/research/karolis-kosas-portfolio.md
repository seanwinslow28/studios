# Karolis Kosas's portfolio (listed 2017, still live)

## What it is

The personal site of Karolis Kosas, a product designer. Its opening line says he works at OpenAI, after Stripe. It is a white page where each project gets one big picture and about 35 words, roughly one project per screen.

He built it himself on Lay Theme, a paid ready-made design kit for portfolios on WordPress, a common website tool. Its badge shows in the corner of every page.

It is here as the plainest real example found of a portfolio that tells a visitor what the person builds inside a minute.

See it: https://karoliskosas.com/

**Best moment: the first screen at desk width.** One line names his job and employers. The first product, a home security device, sits cut off by the right edge of the window with its face blinking, and 29 words beside it. Runner-up: **keep scrolling.** One project per screen, each picture swapping sides and running off the edge.

## How it turned out

- **Quiet, and long-lived.** Siteinspire, a long-running gallery of well-made sites, added it on September 2, 2017. That is the only gallery listing I could find. It has no award from Awwwards or the FWA, the two best-known web design award sites, and no write-up.
- **Only partly current.** The desk page says he is "a product designer at OpenAI, previously Stripe." The phone page says only "a product designer." His About page still says Stripe is where he works now. I did not confirm the OpenAI job anywhere else.
- **The work on show is old.** The home page's picture files were uploaded between March 2017 and May 2020, going by the dated folders in their web addresses. The phones in the pictures are iPhones from about 2014 to 2017. Nothing from Stripe or OpenAI is shown.
- **His experiments got press years ago.** His About page lists It's Nice That, This Is Tomorrow, Creative Applications and The Verge, all for side projects such as an automated zine-printing service. None of that coverage is about this site.
- **The caution:** the reception is modest. This entry is here for its shape, not its fame.

## What makes it good, measured

I loaded the live site on November 18, 2026 in a private test browser, at desk size (1,440 by 900) and phone size (390 by 844). A short script read every piece of text, its size and colour, every picture and where it sits, and the bytes downloaded. I also watched both sizes scroll, frame by frame.

- **What the eye lands on first, and the order after it.** My read, from size, contrast and motion on the first screen, not from eye-tracking:
  1. The opening line, at 72 pixels, top left: the biggest and darkest thing on a white page.
  2. The CUJO security device, bottom right and cut off by the window's edge. It is the only picture on screen and the only thing that moves: a short looping GIF whose eyes open and close.
  3. The project's title, at 48 pixels.
  4. The 29 words about it.
  5. A black, pill-shaped "See more" button.
- **47 words on the first screen:** the 10-word opening line, the 5-word project title and the first 29 words of its description, plus the theme's badge. *Counted: every visible piece of text inside the window.*
- **How long until a visitor could say what he builds.** I can't hold a stopwatch, so I counted the words a reader gets through before each answer, at about 240 words a minute, the average adult pace for silent reading in a 2019 review of 190 reading studies.
  - His job and employers: 10 words, about 3 seconds.
  - One thing he made, the security device and its app: the whole first screen, about 12 seconds.
  - The range (a device, an events app, a cinema-ticket app, a film-streaming service, a studio site): about 190 words and four screens of scrolling, about 50 seconds.
  - So it passes a one-minute test at desk width. What a visitor learns, though, is old work under a new job title.
- **How a piece of work is shown.** On the home page: one still picture per project, a short title and 29 to 39 words, across seven projects. On a project page (I opened MUBI, the film service): a 36-word summary, then "My role" and "Results", three items each, then 11 pictures stacked down the page with no words between them.
- **No decision is shown.** The MUBI page's results read "Increase in paid subscribers and visitors", with no figure and no date. No project shows an earlier version, a choice between options or a change of course.
- **Type: one typeface, one weight, six sizes.** Aeonik, a plain modern sans-serif, is the only font the page loads. At desk width: 72 pixels for the opening line, 48 for project titles, 40 for the writing, talks and email at the foot, 20 for descriptions, 18 for the button label, and 12 for the theme's badge. On a phone: 64, 32, 26, 18 and 16. *Read off the finished page.*
- **Colour: black on white, and almost nothing else.** Pure black and a near-black, #0A0A0A, on white. One grey, #999999, marks the word "Private". All the colour comes from the pictures.
- **A 12-column grid, and pictures that run off the page.** The columns are 120 pixels wide. The words start one column in and are 480 to 600 pixels wide. The pictures are 600 to 840 pixels wide and swap sides from one project to the next. Most run past the edge of the window: one starts 144 pixels off the left edge, and others run 72 pixels past the right. That cropping is what makes a plain page feel generous. *Read from the positions of every text block and picture.*
- **One project per screen.** A new project every 930 to 1,100 pixels. The desk home page is about 11 screens tall: the opening line and first project, six more projects, two screens of archive grid (eight pictures, three across, no gaps), and a last screen of writing, talks and email.
- **Almost nothing moves.** Nothing is tied to the scroll, nothing sticks in place, and there is no parallax, where layers slide at different speeds. The page fades in once when it loads, and links and buttons change in 200 milliseconds. Opening a project simply loads its page.
- **On a phone, every picture on the home page is gone.** One column, 39 pixels from each edge, and tall white gaps where the pictures were. *Checked three ways: no picture elements on the page, zero bytes of pictures downloaded after scrolling to the bottom, and watching it scroll.* Project pages keep their pictures, but at 312 pixels wide the app screens in them are too small to read.
- **Weight.** Desk, before scrolling: 0.77 megabytes in 12 downloads, with the largest paint at 1.6 seconds on this connection. Desk, after scrolling to the bottom: 5.8 megabytes, since pictures load as you reach them. The eight archive pictures alone are about 4.3 megabytes, because they are full-size PNG files, a format that doesn't shrink photos well. Phone: 0.42 megabytes. *Counted from the browser's network log.*
- **A screen reader hears the same description for every picture:** "Karolis Kosas".

### Why it's here

It is the closest real example found of a portfolio built on one rule: one picture per project, with about thirty words beside it. My read on what it teaches a designer: that rule alone can carry a whole page at desk width, with one typeface in one weight, black on white, and pictures big enough to run off the edge. Keep the one line about the job the same on every size and every page.

The cautions: modest reception, old work, and a phone version that drops every picture and turns into an all-words page.
