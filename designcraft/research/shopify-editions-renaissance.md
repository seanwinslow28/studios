# Shopify Editions, Winter '26: "The Renaissance Edition" (2025)

## What it is

Shopify makes the software that runs online shops. Twice a year or so it publishes "Editions", one long web page that presents everything new in the product. Each edition gets a new theme and a new look. There have been ten since summer 2022.

This one came out on December 10, 2025. It presents more than 150 product updates as a walk through a gallery of Renaissance-style paintings built in 3D, which shift and deepen as you scroll. The award entry credits it to Shopify Design, the company's own team. I found no named designers.

It is here as the clearest case of a brand turning its own look on its head. Shop software, dressed as 500-year-old oil paintings.

See it: https://www.shopify.com/editions/winter2026

**Best moment: the top of the page, then the first scroll into the chapter called Sidekick.** That is where the opening painting hands over to the next one. One caveat: I measured this page from its source, not by watching it move. So this pick rests on the awards and on where the page spends its effort, not on my own viewing.

## How it turned out

- **Webby Awards 2026, two wins.** The Webbys are the best-known yearly awards for websites. It won Best Home Page, chosen by the judges, and Best Visual Design (Aesthetic) in the People's Voice public vote.
- **RGD In-House Design Awards 2026, winner and a Judge's Pick.** RGD is a Canadian designers' association. The judge, Nitin Fernandes, praised how the parallax was built. Parallax means layers that move at different speeds as you scroll, which gives a flat picture depth.
- **Shopify's own numbers,** from its award entry: over 3.5 million visits (the entry's word is sessions), 30.5% more than the edition before, with 70% of them on phones.
- **The maker kept the approach.** Earlier editions won Webbys too: Winter '24 in 2024 (a judges' win and a public-vote win) and Winter '25 in 2025. Two more editions have followed this one, in spring and fall 2026.

## What makes it good, measured

I fetched the page source on November 16, 2026 and counted with a short script. Typeface names and colour values come from the page's own stylesheet. How it was made comes from the RGD award entry.

- **Structure:** an opening scene, then 12 chapters: Sidekick, Agentic, Online, Retail, Marketing, Checkout, Operations, Shop app, B2B, Finance, Shipping, Developer.
- **13 painted scenes,** one for the opening and one per chapter. I counted them from the names of the picture files.
- **187 update cards.** Each is a headline and one short paragraph. Headlines are 4 words at the middle of the range and never more than 8. Paragraphs average 16 to 22 words, depending on the chapter. So each idea costs the reader about 25 words.
- **The show is in chapter one.** Sidekick holds all 12 of the page's videos and 257 of its 345 images. The last chapter, Developer, holds 58 cards and is close to a plain list.
- **What the reader can touch:** scrolling drives the motion. There is a jump menu, a search box, video play buttons, and about 21 sample prompts with a button to try each one. The award entry also describes hidden extras: a floating key that unlocks a graffiti layer, and a knitted hat for sale priced after the year Leonardo da Vinci was born.
- **Type, four faces:**
  - HW Cigars, an old-style serif, for headlines and story lines at 20 to 28 pixels.
  - Imperial Script, a formal handwriting face, for 70-pixel drop caps (an oversized first letter) on those headlines.
  - Neue Montreal, a plain bold sans, for the body text of each card at 14 to 16 pixels.
  - Inter, another plain sans, at 13 pixels for the sample prompts, which copy the look of the product's own screens.
- **Colour:** the two most used values in the stylesheet are a cream, #f7f7ee, and an olive black, #292919. The Sidekick chapter keeps that product's violet, #7126ff, on a deep violet, #1c004f.
- **How it was made,** per the award entry: AI drafted each painting. Artists then reworked depth, lighting and the movement of the figures. The motion was tuned in a tool called Theatre.js so that designers, not only engineers, could adjust it. Pictures were compressed so that phones could hold 60 frames a second. The page source backs the last part: every scene has a separate phone version.
- **What was bent:** the whole surface. The pictures, the serif and script type and the cream paper belong to a museum, not a software company.
- **What was kept:** the skeleton. The chapter names and order barely change between editions. The two 2026 editions use 10 or 11 of nearly the same chapters. The card format is the same. A plain sans still carries the body text of every card, and anything that shows the product itself keeps the product's own type.

Against Sean's four story rules:

- **Every headline states its point: mixed.** The four Sidekick headlines are claims, such as "Tedious tasks, simplified". Most of the other 183 are feature names, such as "QR code payments".
- **Plant something early that the close pays off: broken.** My read: it is a catalogue. The theme is the only thread, and the last chapter does not answer the first.
- **One idea per slide: kept.** One update per card, 187 times.
- **The reveal happens in silence: not measured.** The reader sets the pace by scrolling. I could not time it without watching it run.

### Why it's here

It is the nearest thing on the shelf to "take the design system and turn it on its head", and the series has won a Webby three years running, in a different costume each time. My read: the lesson for Designcraft is where the line sits. Keep the skeleton fixed and familiar, then change everything the eye lands on. The caution: the story bones are weak. It is a spectacle wrapped round a list, the opposite of the deck Sean wants to fix.

## What Sean said about it

Seen on November 17, 2026, with the rest of the shelf. This was one of the three the lead recommended, and of those he said: "amazing and very much what I hope Designcraft can produce for me."

He would not name it the house look. Of the whole shelf: "all of the options you provided are amazing in their own way and I wouldn't want to throw those out the window."

He agreed it is the starting point for presentations when a job names no look of its own: "Those will be the starting points to look at."
