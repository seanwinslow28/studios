# Editing and transitions.

Read this when cutting the animatic or the finished piece, deciding how one shot hands over to the next, and timing the whole to music.

## What good looks like.

### Why a cut is right: six reasons, ranked.

A cut is where one shot ends and the next begins. A master editor weighs every cut against six questions, in this order:

1. **Emotion.** Does it give the audience the feeling this moment needs?
2. **Story.** Does it move the story on?
3. **Rhythm.** Does it fall at the right moment in the pulse of the sequence?
4. **Eye-trace.** Where is the viewer looking as the outgoing shot ends, and will their eye find the subject in the incoming one?
5. **The flat screen.** Does the composition hold together across the cut as a two-dimensional picture: left stays left, the line of action keeps its side?
6. **Space.** Does the three-dimensional geography stay consistent: who is where in the room?

Emotion outweighs the other five put together. When something has to give, give up from the bottom. A cut that breaks screen geography but carries the right feeling is the right cut; only a viewer who is not moved will notice the slip. When a cut feels wrong for several reasons, fix the highest one first, because lower faults often vanish behind it. This ranking comes from Walter Murch's thinking on editing, and it holds for animation as well as live action.

### Where a cut falls.

A cut lands best where the thought in the shot completes: the moment a viewer, taking in an idea, would blink. Once a shot stops adding to what the viewer is thinking right now, it is time to leave it. Each shot has only a few such points, and each gives a different reading; a cut between them satisfies none.

Find them at speed. Play the shot in real time and stop on instinct, several times. When your stops keep hitting one frame, that frame is the cut. If the stops scatter, the shot is not yet understood; rethink it rather than scrubbing for a frame.

Other habits that keep cuts clean:

- **Change decisively or not at all.** A small reframe of the same subject jars; a clear change of size or angle reads as a fresh look.
- **Give the eye a landing.** Place the incoming subject near where the eye already was, or allow a frame or two at the head of the shot before the action starts.
- **Do not split one motion across a cut carelessly.** If the incoming shot can carry the whole action, cut just before it begins. A movement chopped in half with frames repeated or skipped stutters.
- **Stage actions to start at the top of a shot.** In animation every frame is made, so start each shot's main action right at its opening frames and the edit has clean cut points.
- **Holding is a choice too.** The number of cuts is no measure of quality. A cut made to look busy weakens every cut around it.

### Rhythm and pace.

Pace follows feeling, and it is felt through contrast. A cutting speed means nothing without its opposite somewhere nearby.

- *Hair Love* cuts every 1.8 seconds during the fight with the hair, then holds one shot for seventeen after the loss.
- The Freeview Play chase cuts every 1.2 to 2.7 seconds, then slows to a ten-second shot under the bridge.
- *Oktapodi* averages 2.2 seconds a shot and speeds up as the van does, to about one cut a second in the cab fight.
- *The Monk and the Fish* averages nine or ten seconds a shot, and its calm is the point.
- BUCK's GitHub explainer runs two speeds: quick deadpan cutting in the live-action opening, then long flying shots once animation takes over.

As rough guides, fast action can run around twenty-five cuts a minute, while calm, conversational material feels normal at six or fewer.

Judge pace at size. On a small preview window, shots feel used up sooner, so cuts creep earlier than they should. Watch at full screen, at full quality, before settling the pace.

### Cutting to music.

Music gives a piece its pulse, but cutting on every beat turns it into a metronome. Land the cuts that matter on downbeats and phrase changes, and let the rest breathe across the bar. The repeated gags in *The Monk and the Fish* land on musical phrases. The *Stranger Things* titles dissolve every 2.84 seconds, evenly, and the steadiness is deliberate there because the piece is about patient assembly.

Let sound lead the picture across a seam. In a J cut, the next shot's sound starts before its picture; in an L cut, the outgoing shot's sound carries on under the new picture. Both smooth a change and pull the viewer forward. A hit of sound works best as a full stop just after the visual lands: the Apple TV chord arrives about a quarter of a second after the logo becomes readable. A gap of silence before the turn makes the turn feel bigger.

### Transitions that carry meaning.

The hard cut is the default and the strongest join. Any other transition must earn its place by saying something a cut cannot.

- **Match cut.** Two shots joined by a shared shape, colour or motion: a round thing becomes another round thing, a gesture finishes in a new place. It tells the viewer the two are connected.
- **Transformation.** One scene becomes the next. In the GitHub explainer, a real rubber duck tossed in the air becomes an animated duck and the camera dives with it into the animated world without a cut. In Ordinary Folk's 2019 Webflow film, each shape turns into the next scene and the first forty-two seconds have no cut at all; the dark world of code tears open into a paper tunnel just as the story turns. In the Freeview film, the blue TV bursts out of a poster.
- **Continuous camera.** When every scene is designed to become the next, a flying camera can carry the whole piece. The cost is the cut's punctuation, so the seams must be designed with real care.
- **The hidden cut.** Something passes close in front of the camera, filling the frame for a moment, and the next clip begins behind it, so several clips read as one long move. This is the way to make generated video feel like a single shot. It works when camera speed and direction match across the seam, the light and colour match, and the passing object fully covers the frame for at least a couple of frames. A jump can also hide on the biggest instant change in light or sound.
- **Dissolve.** Time passing, memory, a gentle change of place.
- **Fade to black.** An ending, or a breath before a new chapter.

Pick one family of transitions for a piece and keep to it. A wipe, spin or glitch that carries no idea is decoration, and decoration between shots reads as noise. A wild transition lands when the story has set it up: when the audience has been given a reason to expect the world to change.

## How it goes wrong, and the fix.

- **Cutting to look busy.** Many cuts, little feeling. Fix: hold, and let fewer cuts do more.
- **Cutting between thoughts.** The hold feels neither long nor short. Fix: move the cut to where the shot's idea lands.
- **A jarring near-match.** Fix: a decisive change of size or angle, or no cut.
- **A lost eye.** The subject leaves top left and the next arrives bottom right in a fast cut. Fix: place the incoming subject near the eye, or give it a frame or two to land.
- **Decorative transitions.** Fix: a hard cut, or a seam designed to carry an idea.
- **A hidden cut that shows.** The speed changes, the light shifts, or the passing object is too brief. Fix: match the move and the grade, and cover the frame fully for longer.
- **A ghost frame at a cut.** With rendered motion blur, a cut placed on a frame's shutter averages both shots into one muddy frame. Fix: place hard cuts midway between frames.
- **A metronome edit.** Every cut on the beat. Fix: cut on phrases, and let some cuts fall off the beat.
- **One pace throughout.** Fix: build contrast, fast against slow, noise against silence.
- **Pace judged on a laptop.** Fix: review at full size and quality before settling it.
- **The wrong sequence blamed.** Viewers point at one stretch as the problem. Fix: look earlier first; the cause is often something they needed to understand before it.

## Examples.

- ***Hair Love*.** Thirty-three cuts in the fight minute, then a long hold and a silence.
- **The Freeview Play film.** A tight chase, then a slow payoff; a half-second near silence at 0:57 marks the move into the heroes' story.
- **BUCK's GitHub explainer.** The duck toss at 0:22 to 0:27 carries the film from live action into animation.
- ***Stranger Things* main title.** Even dissolves, then one unbroken assembly, the music swelling as the logo locks.
