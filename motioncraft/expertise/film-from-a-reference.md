# Making a film from a reference.

Read this when a job starts from a film Sean admires and ours should work like it, or when you study any piece on the shelf to learn what makes it good.

## The idea underneath.

A reference is a goal you measure, not a mood you admire. "Warm and charming" gives you a generic film; "36 shots averaging 3.3 seconds, the chase cut every one to three seconds, then one 10-second hold" gives you a target. The aim is a film as good as the reference and unmistakably ours: its grammar (structure, rhythm, the kinds of transition it uses) carried in our own nouns, never a recognisable sequence copied beat for beat.

A great film spends its energy on three or four showpiece moments held to the highest bar; everything else gets plain good craft. Each extra constraint beyond product truth closes off a bold option.

## What a great study looks like.

### Getting the film.

Work from the best copy you can find, with captions if they exist. Automatic captions time every word, which is gold for the script study, but their starts can drift by a second or two, so trust their counts and durations more. The reference is for study only; it never appears in anything we make.

### Watching it three ways.

1. **Whole, as a viewer.** Before measuring, write down where it lands: the laugh, the turn, the reveal, where energy rises and rests. What the person who chose it loves about it is the strongest guess, and the measurements test these guesses.
2. **As contact sheets at two frames a second,** the whole film on a few sheets, to see the shape: acts, colour fields, scale, where type sits.
3. **Frame by frame at the film's own rate,** around every transition and showpiece, to see how each move is built: the wind-up, the ease, what carries across a cut, the hold.

### What to measure.

- **The skeleton (the words).** Total words, when the voice starts and stops, words a minute per section, where the product's name lands (seconds and share of the film), words on the viewer's problem against the product, the pattern of the how-it-works lines and the air between them, and any running device and its payoff. Paraphrase; never quote the script at length.
- **The beats (the picture).** For each shot: what's on screen, the colour field, the hero object, how it enters and leaves, and what carries into the next shot.
- **Scale and placement.** How tall the hero objects are as a share of the frame, how wide a product card sits, type sizes, where type lives, how much of the frame is empty. Measure in pixels. This is the most common gap between a reference and ours, and the easiest to miss by eye.
- **Motion.** For each technique: what moves, over how many frames, which ease, whether it overshoots, the stagger between items, the hold after landing. Keep what you saw in the frames apart from what you infer about why; a guess treated as fact sends the build chasing it.
- **Transitions.** The idea each seam carries: a shape becomes the next object, a band becomes the interface, an object shrinks to a point that becomes the reveal. A transition that carries an idea is grammar worth taking. A wipe that carries nothing is not.
- **Assets and purpose.** For every asset: the story noun it stands for, its job, what it becomes, and what the story would lose without it.
- **Sound.** When the music enters (often on the name, after a beat of silence), what is silent, which effects mark which picture beats, how loud the voice sits, where cuts land on words.

### Ranking what makes it great.

End with the ten or so things that make the film work, ranked, each with its evidence (times, numbers) and what it means for ours. Then what doesn't transfer (a photographic look we can't carry, a joke only their product earns) and what we'll do instead.

### Comparing films.

When there's more than one reference, or the shelf already holds a studied film, note the principles that recur across them and keep those apart from one film's style. Silence before the turn, or one element held across the seam into the product, is general. A particular red is not.

## Which decisions cost the most to change late, and why.

Each settled decision removes a class of rework, in a natural order.

1. **The files the film must become.** Widescreen only, or square and vertical cuts too, and what lengths. Aspect ratio decides layout and where type can live, so changing it late redraws every frame.
2. **The words.** They set how many beats there are and how long each runs. The reference's measured skeleton is a strong starting shape: the same number of beats, roughly the same word count and pace, the name at about the same point (often 40 to 45 percent in), the voice stopping well before the end. A viewer's problem told in concrete objects and the audience's own words lands harder than description; a how-it-works line works best as a verb the viewer does plus a payoff. Two complete directions, read aloud and timed, make the choice concrete.
3. **The voice, then the music.** The music fixes the clock. Once the sound is settled, every frame has a time, and picture work stops being thrown away.
4. **The beat map, the showpieces and the assets,** placed on that clock: each beat's line, what's on screen, and the idea its transition carries. A motion language written once (one easing family, one timing unit, one transition family, one stagger rhythm) keeps the film speaking with one voice.
5. **The whole film, rough** (every beat present, cheap, low frame rate, no motion blur), on the settled sound. Speed matters more than finish here.
6. **Craft, polish and the finish.** Real assets, full frame rate and motion blur, effects on the picture's cues, then a fresh look against the reference.

The expensive mistake is polishing picture on an unsettled clock: every later change to words or music throws those frames away.

The brand comes in through small moves that never fight the approved look: its colour as the one accent thread, its shapes as motifs, its type in the interface, its end card.

## How it goes wrong, and the fix.

- **Copying the surface instead of the craft.** The film ends up with the reference's colours and none of its rhythm. Fix: copy what the measurements show (pace, scale, holds, transitions) in our own nouns.
- **Copying a sequence beat for beat.** It reads as a knock-off. Fix: keep the kind of move, change what moves. Their object recedes to a point that becomes the logo; ours becomes our product's one transforming element.
- **Measuring the wrong things.** Adjectives instead of numbers, or colour without scale. Fix: measure scale and placement first; a product card at half the reference's size feels timid however good the motion.
- **Reading an upload's timing as the maker's.** A re-encoded copy can carry a different frame rate from the original. Fix: check for repeated frames before trusting any frame count.
- **Settling things late, or out of order.** Looks approved before the words get thrown away when the words change their timing. Fix: words first, then sound, then picture.
- **A script that describes the product.** It reads as a walkthrough. Fix: the benefit goes in the voice and the mechanics go on screen.
- **Decorative assets.** A product card set in a glass block for no reason. Fix: an asset with no story noun and no job comes out.
- **Sliding back to safe at the showpieces.** Fix: name them before the build and judge them hardest.
- **Polish passes that repeat themselves.** Fix: watch both films side by side at matching beats, rank the gaps with times in each, and beat the last pass's findings rather than repeating them.
- **Skipping the study when rushed.** The study is what saves rounds of notes; even in a hurry keep the measured skeleton, scale and placement, the idea each transition carries, and the ranked list.

## Examples.

All on the shelf, `motioncraft/research/`.

- **The Line's Freeview Play film** shows why measuring matters: no spoken words, the chase cut fast and the bridge scene held for 10 seconds, and blue and yellow kept small in area so they pop off a brown town. Its upload runs at 25 frames a second though the studio says it animated at 24, so check the copy before timing it.
- **Stranger Things' main title** has dissolves exactly 2.84 seconds apart and a music swell that peaks as the logo locks. Measured, that's grammar to borrow; admired, it's only "moody".
- **BBC Two's Curve idents** share one shape: still, build, peak, a long ease back to rest, in exactly seven seconds. Only comparing several of them shows that system.
- **Ordinary Folk's Webflow films** let shapes become the next scene so a moving camera stands in for cuts, with brightness alone tracking dark problem to light promise.
