# Sound.

Read this when a job reaches voice, music or effects, and again before the final mix.

## What good looks like.

**Sound is half the picture.** A shot with the right sound feels heavier, faster and more real than the same shot in silence. Animated characters have no sound of their own, so every footstep, squeak and thud is chosen, not recorded. Pick each sound for the feeling it gives first and for literal accuracy second: a cardboard box can land with the thump of a sandbag if the moment needs weight.

**One effect is usually three layers.** A good impact has a hit, a body and a tail. The hit is the sharp front edge, the first few milliseconds that tell the ear exactly when something happened. The body is the middle, the part that says what the thing is made of and how big it is. The tail is the decay, the ring or rumble or room that says where it happened and how hard. A hit alone sounds like a click; a body alone sounds mushy and late. Build important effects from all three, and trim the tail before the next sound needs the space.

**Weight and material live in the sound.** Low frequencies and a longer body read as mass; a bowling ball and a tennis ball differ mostly below the middle of the range and in how long they last. Material lives in the brightness and the ring: glass rings long and high, wood knocks short and dry, cloth and paper hiss and stop, metal keeps sounding after the hit. A heavy settle with a light tap feels wrong even when nobody can say why.

**Few layers at once.** A listener can follow about two and a half distinct things in any five-second moment, and those things should sit in different parts of the range: something low, something in the middle, something musical or high. Choosing which sound to leave out is most of the skill.

**Music sets the pace.** A score tells the audience how fast to feel. A steady pulse carries a sequence of cuts; a pause or a drop makes a moment land. When a piece is driven by music, the music is settled before fine picture timing, because cutting to a beat that later moves wastes the work. Its sections should change where the film's story changes: a turn, a reveal, the end card. A common and strong reveal is half a second of near silence, then the music's full entrance on the product's name or the hero's face.

**Voice pacing matches the reference.** Measure the reference's speed in words per second. A calm explainer sits around two words a second; a fast launch read can reach close to three. When a read runs faster than the reference, space it by lengthening the silences between lines, not by stretching the voice, which smears consonants and sounds processed. Fill each widened gap with quiet room sound taken from that gap, so the air never cuts out. Plan the spacing for the whole film at once, so the total length and the moment the name lands are chosen.

**Silence is a sound.** The most powerful moment in many shorts is a held quiet. It works because everything around it is full, and it needs a reason, usually the story's emotional floor, and enough length to register, often two to four seconds.

**Sync to picture.** The ear catches an error of a couple of frames. Place each effect to within one frame of its cue, and decide for each sound which part lines up with the picture: its start, its loudest point, or its end. A swish aligns its peak with the fastest frame; a door slam aligns its hit with the contact frame. A sound landing a hair after the picture feels natural, because light arrives before sound in life; a sound landing early feels wrong at once. A big chord can wait a quarter-second after a logo becomes readable and feel like a full stop rather than a mistake.

**The web mix is measured, not guessed.** Loudness is the average perceived level of the whole mix, measured in LUFS (loudness units relative to full scale). True peak is the waveform's real highest point, including the peaks that appear between digital samples once the file is played back. The target is set for each job by where the film goes. A web copy aims at about −14 LUFS integrated, with true peak at or under −1 dBTP; most web platforms turn everything to about that level, so a copy mixed there plays at full strength. Broadcast uses −23 LUFS, the European EBU R128 standard. Compression to AAC or MP3 can raise peaks, so the measurement that counts is the one taken on the encoded file itself, not on the WAV it came from.

## How it goes wrong, and the fix.

- **Sound as wallpaper.** Every movement has an effect, the music never stops, and nothing stands out. Fix it by choosing the few moments that matter and giving them room, then taking sounds away until each remaining one reads.
- **Effects vanish under the music.** Effects set to a fixed peak level disappear in busy passages and jump out in quiet ones. Ride each effect against the mix around it, as if moving a fader: a big transition sits a few decibels under the surrounding mix, a small pop well under it, and a little lower again where the voice speaks. When an effect still has to read, open a short pause in the music rather than pushing the effect louder.
- **A logjam.** Every layer works on its own, yet together they turn to mush. Remove anything past about two and a half layers per five seconds, and spread what stays across low, middle and high.
- **Weight that doesn't match.** A heavy body lands with a thin click, or a light prop booms. Rebuild the effect's body and tail to match the animation's mass and material; the hit can usually stay.
- **Music that drifts in tone.** Generated music changes mood when each section is prompted separately. Write one style description, keep it word for word for every take, and change only the structure.
- **Ducking that cuts out.** Music lowered under the voice drops away entirely where the voice ends, because the control signal ends too. Keep the control running to the end of the film, and keep the duck gentle, roughly ten to fourteen decibels under the voice.
- **A pumping mix.** Automatic loudness tools that work in one pass ride the level up and down and make the music breathe audibly. Measure first, then apply one fixed gain, with a limiter only for the few peaks left.
- **Sync drift after a picture change.** A cut moves and every effect after it lands late. Re-read the cue times from the picture and re-place the effects; make new sounds only for new beats.
- **A mix that only works on good speakers.** Quiet detail disappears on a laptop or phone. Check the mix in the format and on the speakers the audience will use, keep quiet detail a little higher than feels right in a small room, and trust the meter when your ears are tired.

## Examples.

- **The Line's Freeview Play film has no voice at all.** One slow cover of a song carries the whole emotional line, with effects underneath. As the film moves from the gloomy town into the heroes' story at 0:57, the sound drops to near silence for half a second. That small gap works like a scene change for the ear.
- **Sean's first film used ten generated sound effects and no music.** With no score to set the pace or cover a gap, each effect had to carry its moment alone, so the hit, body and tail of each sound, its placement on the cue and the quiet around it did all the work music usually shares.
- **Hair Love** goes silent for about 3.7 seconds as the dad is beaten by the hair, and **When the Day Breaks** goes silent for about 3.5 seconds over black at the crash. Both silences are the floor of their films.
- **Oktapodi** takes the opposite road: wall-to-wall score and slapstick effects, with no silence longer than about 0.8 seconds, right for a chase comedy.
- **The Stranger Things title** keeps an almost even synth pattern with no hit on each letter; its one big move is a dip and then a rise of about eight decibels near the end.
- **The BBC Two curve idents** build every ident from the same two notes, so very different films sound like one channel, and in one ident the two loudest sounds sit exactly on the two snaps of the animation.
- **Voice pace on the shelf:** Ordinary Folk's Webflow film runs about 2.3 words a second, Buck's GitHub explainer about 1.9, both with music under the voice throughout.
