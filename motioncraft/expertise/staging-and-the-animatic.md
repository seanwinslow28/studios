# Staging, boards and the animatic.

Read this when beats become shots: thumbnails, boards, the layout of each shot, and the rough timed cut where the piece is judged before anything is made well.

## What good looks like.

### One idea per shot, and a known place for the eye.

Staging is where the subject sits in the frame and where the action happens. A well-staged shot says one thing, and the viewer's eye lands on it at once without being asked. The eye goes to the strongest contrast in value, colour or edge; to whatever moves; to faces and eyes; to the largest or most isolated shape; and along any lines that point. A master uses those pulls on purpose and removes every one that points somewhere else. If a still lamp in the background outshines the moving character, the lamp steals the eye; quiet it.

Let one thing move at a time when the moment matters. Two moving subjects give the eye nowhere to land. The Freeview Play film keeps its heroes small but bright: a blue screen and a yellow coat against brown brick, with the background soft like a photograph of a miniature model. The shallow focus isolates the subject, and the colour finds it.

Stage only what serves the shot. A crowded room tells less than a close shot of the one prop that matters. Empty space around the subject is not waste; it is what makes the subject read.

### Silhouettes that read.

Fill the character in solid black. A strong pose still says what is happening: the reach, the recoil, the peek. Stage actions so the shape is clearest, usually in profile or three-quarter view, with arms and props held clear of the body rather than in front of it. The blue TV peeking from behind boxes reads because its outline changes as it leans out. If a pose only reads with its inside detail, the audience on a phone will miss it.

### Framing, size and angle chosen per beat.

- **Off-centre by default.** A subject on a third of the frame invites the eye to move; dead centre stops it. If a subject must end up centred, start it elsewhere and bring it in. A sequence where every shot is centred and medium puts people to sleep.
- **Depth.** Large foreground shapes against smaller background action make space, and layered planes are the heart of the miniature-set look.
- **Shot size as a story choice.** Wide shots show where we are and how big the world is; close-ups punctuate. Size can reveal or withhold. "Medium" means different things to different people, so name a size by what it holds: the whole TV, the TV's face.
- **Angle as meaning.** The Freeview camera sits low under looming red TVs, so they feel like bullies. *Hair Love* stays at eye level until the fight, then breaks into low angles and a boxing-ring wide, and returns to calm framing after.
- **Camera moves with a job.** Name a move by what the camera does: push in, pull back, pan (turn), track (travel alongside). Keep "zoom" for a lens change. Every move should guide the eye or reveal something.

### Space that stays readable across cuts.

Screen direction is which way things move and face. Picture a line between the two main subjects, or along the path of travel, and keep the camera on one side of it from shot to shot; this is the 180-degree rule. A character leaving frame moving right should enter the next shot still moving right. Crossing the line is allowed when the audience sees why: a shot taken straight down the line, a camera move that crosses on screen, or a wide that re-establishes the space.

Direction also carries feeling. Calm sequences keep movement level or upright; a single diagonal or circular intruder reads as a threat or a turn; directions that change shot to shot read as chaos. Keep three kinds of continuity in mind at once: the world's layout holds (spatial), the story's logic holds (temporal), and travel direction holds (directional).

A camera reveal is a staging problem too. On Sean's first fifteen-second film, a background that faded in on its own read as two scenes glued together. A pull-back only works when the close-up visibly opens into one continuous world.

### Thumbnails and boards.

Thumbnails are tiny, fast, rough drawings, many to a page, used to find the sequence of shots, the composition and the transitions. They stay cheap so nobody defends them. Put them up in order and talk through them aloud, humming the music; leaps in logic and slack stretches show up immediately.

Boards come next: clear, not beautiful, with every prop placed and room in the frame for each action. A stranger should be able to follow the story from the boards alone. Each panel names its beat and what changes. Polish comes last, because early polish wastes drawings.

When the pictures will come from AI image and video models, the same thinking takes a different form. A layout image sets each key frame's framing while a style reference sets the look, and a set roughed out in plain grey 3D boxes fixes the camera move before any finished frame exists. These are the boards; they get the same scrutiny.

### The animatic: where timing problems are cheap.

The animatic is the boards played as a timed film at full length against a scratch soundtrack: temporary music, rough effects, any placeholder sound. It is where the story finally proves itself or doesn't, and the cheapest place to fix it. Trimming a held panel costs a second; trimming a finished shot costs days.

A good animatic is complete, not pretty. Every beat is present, even as a stand-in, and the real music or a close temporary track sets the pace. Holds are counted in frames, not in "a beat". It is watched whole, many times, at full speed and at a size close to the real screen, because a small window makes shots feel used up sooner and invites cutting too early.

What it reveals:

- Beats that drag and beats that rush. Every action needs time to show its preparation, the action itself, and the reaction.
- Geography that confuses: who is where, and which way they are heading.
- A showpiece starved of time, or a filler beat given too much.
- Music fighting the structure.
- A thirty-second idea that actually runs two minutes.

It should feel a little awkward. Resist adding walks, blinks or camera fly-throughs; that is animating, and it hides story problems behind motion.

## How it goes wrong, and the fix.

- **Two focal points.** The strongest contrast sits away from the subject, or two things move at once. Fix: lower the contrast elsewhere, or stagger the moves so one finishes before the next begins.
- **A muddy silhouette.** Limbs or props cross the body and the pose turns into a blob. Fix: turn the character, open the pose, and check it filled black.
- **Screen direction flips.** A subject exits right and enters moving left with nothing to explain it. Fix: restage on one side of the line, or add a shot that carries the crossing.
- **Every shot the same size.** Fix: choose size per beat, wide to place, close to punctuate.
- **A near-match reframe.** Cutting to almost the same framing, just a little closer, jars. Fix: change size or angle decisively, or not at all.
- **The important thing is too small.** A landing staged so wide the squash is a few pixels. Fix: go closer for the beat that carries the feeling.
- **A short, silent or polished animatic.** Fix: full length, scratch sound, rough drawings, holds in frames.
- **Judging a showpiece alone.** It looks great on its own and stalls the sequence. Fix: judge every shot in the cut, beside its neighbours.
- **The story fails muted.** Fix: restage until the picture alone tells it.

## Examples.

- **The Freeview Play film.** Low angles under looming TVs, soft backgrounds that isolate small bright heroes, and a ten-second shot under the bridge that gives the payoff room.
- ***The Monk and the Fish*.** Locked-off wide shots with the monk often tiny in frame, yet always readable: the watercolour world holds still, only the characters move, and strong drawn perspective does the work a camera move would.
- ***Hair Love*.** Calm eye-level staging broken deliberately for the fight, with a palette that drops to near-black and one spotlight.
- **Ordinary Folk's 2019 Webflow film.** One small glowing ball travels through nearly every scene, so the eye always knows where to look.
- ***When the Day Breaks*.** Colour so rationed that whatever is in colour becomes the focus of the frame.
