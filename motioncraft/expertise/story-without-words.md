# Story, and telling it without words.

Read this when a piece needs a story: at the front door while the idea is found, when the beats are laid out, and whenever a rough cut looks lovely but feels empty.

## What good looks like.

### One idea, small enough to say in a breath.

A short motion piece, anywhere from five seconds to ninety, carries one idea. A master can say it in a single sentence that holds the tone, what happens, and what it is really about. The Freeview Play film is "television without subscriptions" told as a fable about a shy blue TV finding friends in a town of bossy red ones. Nothing in two minutes is spent on anything else. If the idea will not fit in a sentence, or the maker cannot yet say it out loud, there is no story yet, only a look or a mood, and boarding will not find one.

What the piece is really about, its theme, does practical work. When two endings compete, the theme picks the one that proves it. A hopeful theme and a bleak one make opposite endings from the same events.

### A character who wants something.

Give someone a want and something in the way, and an audience will follow them anywhere. A product or an idea earns its place by answering that want, not by being listed.

The Freeview film never shows a menu or names a feature. The girl, the dog and the old man are each stuck at home with a bossy red TV; the blue TV is hiding. The product is the blue TV and the end card, and the argument lands harder for it. The same move works for any launch: find whose day the product changes, give that person a small, concrete want, and let the product arrive as the turn. Everything the character does not need goes.

### Beats, and a shape.

A beat is the smallest change in the story: something physical or emotional shifts. A panel where nothing changes is not a beat, it is a hold, and its length is a choice. A short film of a few minutes runs on fifteen to thirty beats; a sixty-second spot on perhaps ten; a five-second sting on two. The Apple TV sting is exactly two: a fast move, then a long settle.

The classic shape is a character with a problem, attempts that make it worse until it looks hopeless, and a short, surprising solve. The last part is the shortest. Other shapes suit other material:

- **Book end:** open on an image and close on it again, changed.
- **String of moments:** a strong piece of music carries a run of linked scenes. *The Monk and the Fish* is built on a Baroque theme played over and over with growing variation, and the chase repeats the same way, each time a little stranger.
- **Countdown:** an event that must happen, and the tension of waiting for it.
- **Puzzle:** a late reveal that reframes everything. *Hair Love* withholds where the father and daughter are going until the end.
- **High concept:** a one-line premise anyone can picture at once.

Open inside the problem. The Freeview film spends nine seconds on a film-style opening, then goes straight to the red TVs rallying under "subscribe" banners. *Oktapodi* sets up its two lovers and splits them in about twenty seconds, but holds its first shot for thirteen of them so we care before the chase begins.

### Setup and payoff.

What is planted early is paid off late, and the payoff closes a loop the audience did not know was open. The old man in the Freeview film starts alone in his flat with a red TV and ends walking out at sunrise with a child. The monk's endless grabbing at the fish turns, in the last half minute, into the two of them rising into the sky together. The best payoff is often the first object returning, changed.

A short has room for three or four showpieces: moments held to the highest bar. Usually they are the problem piling up, the turn, the one transforming element, and the payoff. Everything else gets craft, not flash.

## Telling it with no voice.

### The picture carries the meaning with the sound off.

Watch the piece muted. If a beat only works with sound, the sound is doing the acting and the picture has failed. The test comes from the old idea of a short told in pantomime: if an action is missed, so is the feeling it carried. The makers of *Hair Love* left out dialogue on purpose so the story would rest on acting alone.

### Acting by contrast.

Wordless performance reads through difference. In *Hair Love* a big, gentle father slumps and braces against a quick, springy daughter. The monk crouches heavily before every lunge while the fish pops out of the water with no weight at all. In *Oktapodi* soft, rubbery octopuses fight a stiff, bony driver. Simple faces carry more than detailed ones: the blue TV's face is two curved lines on a screen, and it still reads as shy, then hopeful.

### Colour argues.

Colour can make the case a voice would. The Freeview villains are red and black; the heroes are screen blue and coat yellow, small in area against a brown town, which is exactly why they stand out. The last fifteen seconds turn gold at sunrise. Plan colour as one bar per key beat, and at each key moment pull one lever: a change of hue for danger, darker values for fear or loss, saturation rising as joy returns. Allow one surprise colour at most. *When the Day Breaks* is nearly grey all through, so a lemon or a red cross is impossible to miss.

### Sound as narrator, silence as the loudest moment.

With no voice, music carries the emotional line and the effects point at what matters. Plan sound while writing, not after picture. Halve the list of effects you think you need; a sound that suggests a feeling beats a literal one.

Silence is the strongest tool on the soundtrack. *Hair Love* drops out for nearly four seconds as the father loses to the hair. *When the Day Breaks* goes silent over black for three and a half seconds at the crash. The Freeview film dips to near silence for half a second as it leaves the gloomy town for the heroes' story. Each silence marks the turn.

### Show the trace, not the event.

Suggestion draws an audience in; explanation pushes them back. *When the Day Breaks* never shows the crash. We see a car grille, black, then lemons rolling across the road, a pair of glasses, a hat spinning to a stop. One minute, told only through objects. Pace tells too: *Hair Love* cuts every 1.8 seconds through the fight, then holds one shot for seventeen after the loss.

## How it goes wrong, and the fix.

- **A look with no story.** A gorgeous world and forgettable characters. Fix: write the one-sentence idea before any image, and keep only the world's rules that create obstacles for the character.
- **A feature parade.** The piece walks through the product. Fix: find the person whose day it changes, give them a want and an obstacle, and let the product be the answer. Cut every feature that character does not need.
- **A slow opening.** Viewers ask what is going on. Fix: start later. Cut beats from the front until the first one holds a problem.
- **Nothing gets worse.** The hero's luck stays flat, so there is no drama. Fix: sketch their fortune over time and make the middle beats escalate, ideally from the character's own traits.
- **A payoff with nothing planted.** The ending arrives from nowhere, or two endings compete. Fix: let the theme choose, then plant small clues for it early.
- **Sound doing the acting.** The muted watch loses the thread. Fix: restage until the action reads in picture alone.
- **Everything at full volume.** A crash on every beat, colour everywhere. Fix: ration. A climax lands only against restraint.
- **Too much story for the time.** Fix: time beats in seconds early; cut even loved ones. *Oktapodi* was made to its school's limit of about two minutes; every shot has one job.
- **A title card that explains.** Fix: find an object or an action that shows it instead.

## Examples.

- **The Freeview Play launch film (The Line, 2015).** Two minutes, no voice. Setup to 0:36, characters to 1:05, the turn as the blue TV breaks out of a poster, a chase, then the payoff under the bridge at sunrise. Best moment: 1:38 to 1:46.
- ***Hair Love* (2019).** Wordless acting built on contrast. Best moment: 2:00 to 3:06.
- ***The Monk and the Fish* (1994).** A repeating chase built like its music, ending in one gesture that lets it all go. Best moment: 5:20 to 5:56.
- ***When the Day Breaks* (1999).** A death told through objects and silence. Best moment: 3:24 to about 4:25.
- ***Oktapodi* (2007).** A love story and chase in two minutes, set up in twenty. Best moment: 0:46 to 1:03.
