# Timing, spacing and weight.

Read this whenever something moves: a character, a seed, a logo, a card, a word. The same fundamentals govern all of them.

## What good looks like.

### Timing and spacing are two controls.

Timing is how long a move takes, counted in frames. Spacing is where the object sits on each of those frames: positions close together read slow, positions far apart read fast. The same twelve frames can feel heavy, light, nervous or lazy depending only on the spacing. When a move feels wrong, check the two separately; most problems live in one of them. A quick plan: move a plain dot along the path first.

In motion graphics the easing curve is the spacing and the duration is the timing. Treating them as two decisions is what separates designed motion from a preset.

### Slow in and slow out, said by pose and direction.

Near a pose the spacing bunches up, so the move slows as it arrives or leaves. Naming the pose and the direction, "slow into the rest" or "fast out of the anticipation", says exactly which end of the move bunches up. Bare "ease in" and "ease out" mean opposite things in different programs, so they often produce the wrong curve.

Not every move slows at both ends. A falling object speeds up all the way into the floor. A move that slows into and out of every pose, the default in most tools, makes everything float at one polite speed.

### Arcs.

Living things move on curves: a head turn dips in the middle, a bounce traces a clean parabola. Trace the path across neighbouring frames: a kink, a wobble or a dip through the floor is a mistake the eye will catch at full speed. Straight lines belong to machines.

### Anticipation.

Before a move, a small move the other way, slower than the action: a crouch before a jump. It prepares the eye and sells the force. For a snappy move it can be only one to three frames. The same size of anticipation on every move turns corny; size it to the action.

### Overlap and follow-through.

Parts don't start or stop together. Something leads and the rest drags: hips before head, elbow before hand. Two parts doing the same thing should be offset by a few frames, about four at 24 a second. When a figure stops, every part carries on a little past the stop and comes back in its own time, with loose things like hair, cloth, ears or a tail last. Everything arriving on one frame looks dead.

### Squash and stretch.

Under force a shape changes, but its volume stays the same: as it squashes it gets wider, as it stretches it gets thinner. How far something squashes says what it is made of: a rubber ball a lot, a seed a little, a stone hardly at all. Squash belongs on the contact frame. Stretch belongs on the fast frames just before and after it and is never held. Used everywhere, squash and stretch makes everything look rubbery.

### Contact and settling.

Weight is sold around the moment of contact.

- **Into the contact,** the spacing keeps widening. A landing that slows into the floor reads as hovering.
- **On the contact,** one frame touches the ground undistorted, then the squash; on a very fast hit, skip the touching frame and go straight to the squash.
- **On the rebounds,** each is lower and shorter than the last, and the spacing coming up out of the floor is tighter than the spacing going in, which shows energy lost. Rebounds that die quickly read heavier.
- **Into the rest,** spacing bunches at the top of each rebound and again into the final pose, and the parts come to rest one by one.

Any big change of tilt or shape between two neighbouring frames at a landing reads as a snap. Spread it over three or four frames. Sound belongs on the frame just after the touch.

### Smears as designed poses.

When two positions sit so far apart that the eye sees separate pictures, the motion strobes. A smear is one designed frame that stretches the object along its path from the outer edge of one key to the outer edge of the next, built from the object itself, with the same colours and the same volume. It lasts one frame, shown on ones (a new image every frame), followed by one or two frames that fold into the new pose. Held for two frames, or shown on twos (each image held for two frames), it stops reading as speed and reads as a strange drawing. A blur filter is not a smear. Smears do not belong in slow moves, holds or contacts, and they cannot rescue a bad pose; fix the pose first.

### The falling-object test.

To find out whether a thing has weight, drop it. Does it gather speed? How far does it squash? How many times does it bounce, how fast do the bounces die, and does it visibly come to rest? Heavy things accelerate hard, squash little, rebound low and stop fast. Light things drift, flutter and take a long time to settle. If an object can't fall convincingly, its weight hasn't been designed yet, logo or character.

### Holds and moving holds.

A hold needs about six frames to read at all; shorter looks like a mistake. An expression needs about twelve. A drawn hold can sit perfectly still, because the drawing itself has life: *The Monk and the Fish* holds poses for about half a second. A 3D or vector hold that stops dead looks frozen, so give it a moving hold: a slight drift, a breath, or one part still overlapping into the pose.

Hold length is reading time. The Apple TV sting moves fast for a second and a half, drifts to rest by three seconds, then sits dead still for two, so the logo can be read. The BBC Two "Squish" ident keeps its curve clean and still for about a third of each loop.

### Ones and twos.

Hand-drawn acting usually runs on twos; fast action, smears and anything under a camera move go on ones, or they strobe. 3D and code-built motion runs on ones by default and tends to float, so push the key poses harder and leave bigger gaps in the spacing.

## In motion graphics and type.

- **Name every curve by its poses.** The Apple TV sting is fast out of the start and long and slow into the rest, with no bounce or overshoot. The BBC Two "Squish" ident has two sharp moves, each followed by a long, slow settle.
- **Give type weight on purpose.** A heavy word lands with little or no overshoot and stops quickly. A light one drifts in. *Caption with Intention* swells a shouted word to twice its height for about a quarter second, then relaxes over another quarter to a third, with no bounce.
- **Stagger, don't sync.** Letters, words and cards arrive a few frames apart. A label or shadow trails the card it belongs to.
- **Overshoot only when it means something.** A bounce on every element is the clearest sign of preset motion.
- **Let organic elements travel on a slight arc.** Keep straight lines for things that should feel engineered.

## How it goes wrong, and the fix.

- **Floaty motion.** Even spacing or a default curve everywhere. Favour the key poses and open up the gaps between them.
- **A mechanical slide.** Even spacing from end to end. Bunch the spacing near the poses that matter.
- **Everything is rubbery.** Squash and stretch everywhere. Keep it to contacts and fast frames, sized to the material.
- **Everything arrives at once.** Stagger the parts and let loose ones settle last.
- **Too fast to read.** Double the timing first, then trim.
- **Metronome timing.** Every move the same length. Mix long and short, fast and slow.
- **A dead hold.** Add a moving hold, or cut away sooner.

## Example: the seed in Sean's first film.

The seed's performance in Sean's first film is all of this in about four seconds. It hangs still, then makes a small counter move before it lets go: the anticipation. It tumbles and turns on the way down, slower than a stone because it is light and catches the air, but still gathering speed so its spacing opens toward the floor. On the contact frame it squashes, growing wider as it grows shorter. It bounces twice, the second bounce lower and quicker than the first, each rising slower than it fell. Then it slows into the rest and sits visibly still before it plants itself. Every bounce earns its place by saying something about what the seed is.

Earlier attempts showed the two classic failures. A landing that slowed into the floor read as hovering. One whose tilt jumped sharply between two frames read as a snap. The fix for both lies in the spacing and the arc, not in more detail.
