# The Line's "Set Yourself Free" launch film for Freeview Play (2015)

## What it is

The launch film for Freeview Play, a free on-demand TV service in the UK. It put catch-up TV from the main British channels in one place, with no subscription. First aired October 2015, 2 minutes, plus three shorter companion films.

Made by The Line, a London animation group (six people at the time), with director Sam Brown. Full credits are under "More details".

There's no voiceover. A slow cover of "I Dreamed a Dream", sung by Sarah Kingsmill, carries it. The story: in a town where red-screened TVs push everyone to subscribe and upgrade, a shy blue-screened TV, a girl in a yellow coat, her dog and an old man find each other.

Watch: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WgAazxaGST8 (The Line's own upload). The studio's page has a making-of: https://thelinestudio.com/work/freeview-set-yourself-free

**Best moment: 1:38 to 1:46.** The blue TV peeks out from behind cardboard boxes under a railway bridge. Its whole face is two curved lines on a blue screen. It still reads clearly as shy, then hopeful.

## How it turned out

- **Freeview's biggest ad campaign to that date,** according to the industry site shots and Digital TV Europe at launch.
- **Trade press.** Stash featured it in October 2015. It reported a 10-month production, 45 artists, and 25 characters plus hundreds more in crowd scenes. Shots called the spot "charming".
- **Scale, from the studio.** The Line says it made over 700 character designs before production and built a whole British town from scratch. It was the studio's biggest ad job up to then.
- **The studio's standing.** The Line's first short film was nominated for a BAFTA in 2014. That nomination is what brought brands to them.
- **What I couldn't find:** no award for this film. The studio's YouTube upload has only about 8,800 views, because this was a TV campaign first. Its reception evidence is thinner than the other two picks. It's here for the craft and the storytelling.

## What makes it good, measured

I measured these from the 1080p YouTube upload with ffmpeg scene detection (which finds hard cuts) and frame grabs. Making-of facts come from The Line's page and Stash.

- **Length and frame rate:** 120.1 seconds. The upload runs at 25 frames a second, the UK TV standard. The Line says it was animated at 24.
- **Shots:** 36 shots, averaging 3.3 seconds. Most are 1 to 4 seconds. The longest are the opening (8.7 seconds) and two slow emotional beats near the end (8 to 10 seconds).
- **Pacing you can feel:** the chase (1:16 to 1:30) cuts every 1.2 to 2.7 seconds. Then it slows right down for the bridge scene, with one 10-second shot.
- **One idea, zero spoken words.** TV without subscriptions, told as a fable. The product appears only as the blue TV and the end card. No UI at all.
- **Colour does the arguing.** The bad guys are red and black: red-screened TVs, red banners. The heroes get two colours that pop off the brown and brick town: screen blue and coat yellow. The last 15 seconds turn warm and golden at sunrise. Measured main colours are dark browns (#201314, #3d2624, #5f3f39) and a warm cream (#d5c7b7). The blue and yellow are small in area, which is why they stand out.
- **Camera and look:** low angles under looming TVs. Blurry backgrounds, like photos of a miniature model. That matches the studio's stated aim: a model world built inside the computer, with stop-motion imperfections. It's 3D, but it looks hand-made, the opposite of a slick video-game look.
- **Sound:** one song does all the emotional work. Measured: a half-second drop to near silence at 0:57, as the film moves from the gloomy town into the heroes' story.
- **Structure with timings:**
  - Overture, 0:00 to 0:09: "Freeview Play presents", over a rainy puddle, like the start of a film.
  - Setup, 0:09 to 0:36: red TVs hold rallies under "subscribe" and "upgrade" banners. The blue TV hides.
  - Characters, 0:36 to 1:05: the girl, the dog and the old man, each stuck at home with a bossy red TV.
  - Turn, 1:05 to 1:16: the blue TV breaks out of a "Future of TV" poster as the red TVs march.
  - Chase, 1:16 to 1:30.
  - Payoff, 1:30 to 1:53: under the bridge they meet. Sunrise. The old man walks out with a child.
  - End card, 1:53 to 2:00.

### Why it's here

It proves a product launch can be a pure character story, with no features and no screens explained. It's also a useful contrast with work that looked like "an old PlayStation game": 3D made to feel like a hand-built miniature set.
