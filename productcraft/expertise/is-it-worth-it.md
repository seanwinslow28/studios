# Is this idea worth it.

Read this when Sean brings an idea and wants to know whether it deserves his evenings before anything gets built, or when something he started has gone quiet and he's unsure whether to keep going. A good call is short and countable: what we guessed, what we tested, what people did, and whether that cleared a bar set before we looked. How to run the conversations themselves is in the brief on talking to customers.

## The bar: ten people who'll pay.

Jason Cohen's rule is the studio's starting point. Before an idea earns real building time, find ten people from the market you're aiming at who say, in effect, "build this and I'll pay you this much." Ten, a specific price, and not friends. He wrote it in 2010 after seeing hundreds of pitches at an Austin startup program, where almost none had reached ten.

Why it works:

- **You can count it.** "People seem interested" can't fail. Seven out of ten can.
- **It throws out cheap signals.** Compliments, likes, a friend who loves it and "I'd use that" all fall short. Only a stated willingness to pay counts.
- **It's small enough to reach.** If millions of people really want this, ten should be easy. If ten are hard to find, that's the answer.
- **It aims at the real risk.** For most small products the code isn't what fails; finding buyers is.

Cohen says 30 people agreed to $50 a month for WP Engine before it had a name. That's his own account, told slightly differently across his essays: a story about the method, not a target.

Ten yeses are still words, not actions. They clear the bar for spending real time, not for calling the business proven. The stronger tests below sit on top.

## Write the guesses down first.

Before the first conversation or test, write down what you believe, one guess per line: who the customer is, what hurts, how they cope today, what they'd pay, what makes them buy now. Make each specific enough to be wrong ("a bookkeeper with ten clients would pay $30 a month"), not a mood ("small businesses want this").

The list shows what to ask and keeps you honest, since you can't later claim you knew all along. Cohen wrote 18 sensible guesses before WP Engine and about half were wrong. One he thought obvious, that people want a free trial, was false for web hosting: moving a site is such a chore that a free move sold better.

Split guesses that hide two claims: "they'll buy it and tell friends" is two tests. Then test first the guesses that would kill the idea if wrong and have no real evidence yet. And let the guesses be Sean's. A list a model wrote for him isn't his prediction, so it can't surprise him.

## Ask for money, with a real number.

Price is part of the idea, not a detail for later. Cohen's favourite range for a small self-funded product is around $100 a month, where 200 to 300 customers can make it profitable. At $10 a month, he notes, it takes over 8,000 customers just to reach $1 million a year. Those are different businesses with different customers.

So bring a real price in early. Cohen floated $50 a month near the end of his interviews. One group thought it outrageous; another said they wouldn't trust it if it were cheaper. That split showed him two kinds of customer he'd otherwise have lumped together.

- **State a price and watch the reaction** rather than asking "what would you pay?" People guess low or say what sounds polite. If everyone agrees instantly, it's probably too low.
- **Anchor on what they already spend.** Money, hours and hassle on today's workaround set the floor; what the product is worth to them sets the ceiling. Your build cost isn't part of the price.

## Watch what people do: demand tests.

What people do outweighs what they say, and a big commitment outweighs a small one. An email address is a small yes; picking a paid plan is bigger; a card number or deposit is bigger still.

Buffer is the model of a cheap test. Joel Gascoigne put up two pages before writing any product: one describing the idea, one asking for an email. Then he slotted a pricing page between them, so people had to pick a plan first. One extra click tested whether interest survived a price, and which plan. Paying customers arrived within days of launch.

The cheapest tests first:

- **A page and a waitlist** (a page the lead builds plus a Tally form) tests whether the words land.
- **A price on that page** tests whether interest survives money.
- **A Stripe payment link or preorder** tests whether they'll pay now. Stripe takes 2.9% plus 30 cents a payment, and refunds are simple. Say plainly that it's early.
- **Doing the service by hand,** with no software, tests whether the result is worth paying for and shows exactly what to build.

Pick the strongest test time allows, and no paid ads before a free waitlist shows interest.

## Is it big enough to bother.

A test can pass and the idea still not be worth it. Do the rough sum before building: the price, how many paying customers make it worth Sean's time, and how many people he'd need to reach to get them, remembering most visitors never buy. If the count is absurd, look for one big lever, usually a higher price or a narrower customer who hurts more, before giving up.

Sean builds on the side, so his hours are the real cost. Cap them at a set number a week rather than pretending they're free, and don't judge the first months by revenue per hour, which always looks silly early.

## Kill criteria: draw the line before the test.

Decide what failure looks like before running anything, in counts: "at least 4 of 10 pick a paid plan," not "a good share are interested." Then hold to it. Set 4, get 3, and the guess failed. You can rerun it; you can't relabel it.

Superhuman shows the habit on a product with users. The team took Sean Ellis's bar before measuring: at least 40% of active users should say they'd be "very disappointed" without it. They started at 22%, reached 33% by narrowing to the right users, then 58%. Because the bar came first, the number meant something.

Cohen adds a test of shape. When answers keep pointing at the same customer, pain and price, the idea is converging. Before WP Engine he tried an idea everyone praised, yet each person sent him somewhere different: enterprise at $1,000 a month, freemium at $5, small business, ad measurement. Praise that scatters is a no.

## When to stop: commit or quit.

Every check ends in one of three calls: commit, change one big thing and test again, or stop. "Keep tinkering" isn't one.

Nathan Barry's ConvertKit had slid to about $1,200 a month by late 2014. He laid out three options, shut it down, leave it running unattended, or go all in, and went all in: full time, a small team, $50,000 of his savings. The real change was narrowing to professional bloggers. It reached $5,020 a month by March 2015.

The Browser Company's letter about Arc shows the other side: a middle path (security fixes only, no new features), the numbers behind it (only 5.52% of daily users regularly used more than one Space, a core feature), and an admission they'd waited a year too long. Most stops come late. When the evidence already says no, waiting is the expensive choice.

## Where it goes wrong.

- **Counting polite yeses.** Fix: only commitments count.
- **No price in the test.** Fix: a real number on the page or in the call.
- **Guesses written after the results.** Fix: written and dated first, in Sean's words.
- **The bar set after looking.** Fix: counts decided in advance and held to.
- **One test, then build.** Fix: a stated yes, then a test where people act.
- **Scattered answers read as "everyone likes it".** Fix: check whether customer, pain and price are narrowing.
- **Tinkering instead of deciding.** Fix: commit, change one thing, or stop, with a date for the call.

## What a strong product leader would notice.

- No price anywhere in the test.
- Every piece of evidence is something people said, nothing anyone did.
- A pass bar that appeared after the results.
- Friends and family in the count.
- A customer described as "everyone who..."
- Answers that agree it's nice but disagree on who, what and how much.
- An idea that has been "almost ready to test" for weeks.
