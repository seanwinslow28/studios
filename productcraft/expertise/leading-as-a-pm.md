# Leading as a product manager.

Read this when a job is about how a product manager works, not what gets built: a decision memo for day-job work, a stuck disagreement, a team handed a feature list, a hard conversation. Most of it serves the day job; some applies to Sean's own products, where the person he most often has to say no to is himself.

A strong product manager is judged by what changed for customers and the business, not by what shipped or how full the calendar was.

## Owning the outcome.

Owning the outcome means treating the result as yours when most of the work is other people's and none of them report to you. An owner says "the launch slipped because I didn't get the pricing call made in time", not "legal was slow". Ben Horowitz's old training note for Netscape's product managers is mostly this contrast: the strong one sets the target and takes the blame, the weak one has a reason ready for every miss.

Owning isn't doing everything. It's making sure the decision gets made, the risk gets named early, and the team knows why. Marty Cagan frames it as owner against employee: an owner answers for results, an employee for tasks done.

Shreyas Doshi adds a useful suspicion: a team's "execution problem" is often a strategy, trust or people problem in disguise. An owner checks before asking for more engineers.

## Writing is the job.

Most of a product manager's influence travels on paper. A clear page lets people who weren't in the room understand the call and disagree with something specific. Writing also shows up thin thinking: bullets can hide a missing argument, a paragraph can't.

- **The decision memo.** The first lines say what needs deciding and by when. Then the real options, doing nothing included, the recommendation and why, the cost, and what would change the writer's mind. Amazon's habit, described in *Working Backwards*, is the strong version: full prose, read in silence at the start of the meeting, so everyone argues from the same facts.
- **The one-pager.** For a smaller bet: the problem, who has it, how we'll know it worked, the biggest risk, and what we're choosing not to do. If it won't fit on a page, the thinking isn't finished.

Good product writing puts the ask first, uses numbers where it's tempted to use adjectives, and names its own weak spot before a reader does. The Arc letter on the shelf shows the usage numbers behind stopping new features on the browser and admits the call came a year late.

## Give teams problems, not features.

An empowered product team is handed a problem and judged on whether it got solved. A feature team is handed a list and judged on whether it shipped. Cagan's *Empowered*, written with Chris Jones, argues the first makes far better products: the engineers and designers nearest the technology often see the best answer, and people care more about problems they own.

A quick test is to count the answers an ask allows. "Add a spreadsheet export" allows one. "Finance teams can't get our numbers into their own reports" allows many, and one may beat the export.

The signs of a feature team: engineers meet the work only once it's scheduled, and launches get celebrated but never measured. John Cutler called this a feature factory in 2016. Problems still need edges, though. "Improve retention" is too big to move in a quarter; the Netflix example on the shelf shows how to give each team a number it can actually shift.

## Spend time where it multiplies.

Andy Grove, in *High Output Management*, counts a manager's output as what their own group produces plus what the groups they influence produce. So the question isn't "was I busy?" but "which hour moved the most?"

Doshi's LNO idea gives this a shape. Leverage tasks (the strategy, the key memo, the hard decision) pay back many times over and deserve your best. Neutral tasks deserve a decent job. Overhead, like most email, deserves just enough. The usual failure isn't laziness; it's polishing everything equally while avoiding the one hard leverage task.

Saying no is the same skill pointed outward. Ken Norton argues the real danger is a vague answer, not an argument. "We'll look into it" feels kind and is the worst reply: months later, nothing has happened. A clear no, with the reason and what would change it, is the respectful answer. Every yes is a no to something else, and a strong PM says what. Apple's 1997 cuts, on the shelf, are no at company scale: fifteen desktop models down to one.

## Influence without authority.

Product managers rarely manage the people who build. They lead by being right often, being clear, and being trusted. Doshi is blunt: a PM short of influence usually needs sharper judgment or better persuasion, not more formal power. A master:

- **Does the homework,** and always gives the reasons, so people make good calls without them.
- **Brings people in early.** Nobody fights for a plan they first saw finished.
- **Speaks for whoever isn't there:** the customer, support, the team who'll maintain it.
- **Forces the decision.** No decision is worse than a no. When peers are stuck, escalating is what managers exist for; Norton's advice is to lay out both sides fairly and put a price on waiting, in people blocked and customers affected.
- **Disagrees, then commits,** and backs the call in public.

## Managing up and across.

Managing up means your manager is never surprised. Bad news goes early, with what you're doing about it. Norton describes how contact between leaders thins as a company grows until only fires get discussed; the topic that matters but isn't burning yet is the one to raise now. Learn what your boss feels most strongly about and settle those questions before work starts.

Managing across means peers in sales, support, finance, legal and design. Show them plans before building, so their concerns shape the work instead of stopping it late. Treat legal and security as advisors whose risks you weigh, not gates you queue at.

Doshi's three levels help when an argument circles: execution (built well?), impact (does it matter?) and optics (how is it seen?). A PM proud of shipping against the odds and a VP saying it isn't good enough are often arguing at different levels. Naming that ends the debate.

## Meetings and one-on-ones.

For Grove, a meeting is a tool shaped by its job. A regular meeting shares information; a one-off meeting exists to reach a decision and stands or falls on the preparation. Claire Hughes Johnson's *Scaling People* adds two habits: say aloud how the decision will be made and who makes it, and have the highest-ranking person give their view last, so nobody just echoes the boss. If a written update would do, skip the meeting.

A one-on-one belongs to the other person. Hold it regularly, don't cancel it, let them bring the agenda, and listen far more than you talk. Norton adds: don't end it early, because what matters often surfaces once the updates run out.

## Coaching.

For a leader with reports, coaching is the main job. Cagan's method is a gap check, not a review: which skills matter most at this person's level, where they are now, and which two or three gaps to work on this quarter. A good tool is asking them to write their reasoning down, then talking it through.

Grove has a handy way to tell skill from will: imagine the person's life was at stake. Could they do the task then? If yes, it's motivation. If no, it's skill, and teaching will fix it where pressure won't. A surprise at review time is the manager's failure.

## Where it goes wrong.

- **The busy update.** Fix: say what changed, what's next, and what you need.
- **The feature list handed down.** Fix: rewrite each item as the problem it solves and let the team propose answers.
- **"We'll look into it."** Fix: a clear yes, no or not-now, with the reason.
- **A meeting with no decision.** Fix: put the decision wanted in the invite, or cancel.
- **The memo that buries the ask.** Fix: the first two lines say what needs deciding and by when.
- **Rescuing.** Taking work back from someone struggling fixes today and teaches nothing. Fix: coach them through it.

## Examples.

- **Horowitz's "Good Product Manager / Bad Product Manager"**: about fifteen pairs of strong and weak habits side by side, each one visible within a week. The model for how this studio teaches.
- **Apple's 1997 cuts**: no at company scale, every cut serving one plan.
- **The Arc letter**: a public decision memo with numbers and an admission of lateness.
- **Netflix's proxy metrics**: problems with numbers teams could move.

## What a strong product leader would notice.

- Whether the first two lines say what's being decided, and by when.
- An update made of activities instead of results.
- A team handed a solution where it needed a problem.
- A yes with nothing named as given up.
- A meeting with no named decision-maker.
- A miss explained only by other people.
- An argument about details that is really about shipping against impact.
