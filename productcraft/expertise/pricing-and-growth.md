# Pricing and growth.

Read this when a job touches money or reach: what to charge, how to package it, where the first users come from, and how a product keeps growing. A one-person side product needs a price and one way to be found. Day-job work asks the same questions of a bigger product. Either way, price and growth are product decisions made early, not marketing chores bolted on at the end.

## Price is decided before the product.

Most products get built, then priced. The master does it the other way round: find out what people will pay, then design what they'll pay for, then build it. Madhavan Ramanujam and Georg Tacke's *Monetizing Innovation* rests on that order, and on the authors' argument that new products fail far more often on the money side than the engineering side.

Asking about price early tests the idea (a yes to a real number means they have the problem), shapes the product (what people would pay extra for is the build order), and sorts the customers (groups who value it differently may need different versions).

For a side product with no users, this is Jason Cohen's rule from the shelf: before building, find ten people who say they'll pay a stated price. Not "would you use this?", but "it's $20 a month; would you pay that?" Buffer showed the clicked version: a page of plans with prices before the email box.

## Finding what people will pay.

People can't name a price from thin air but react well to one. Give them numbers.

- **Ask around a range.** At what price is this a bargain? Getting expensive? Too expensive to consider? The answers draw a rough curve.
- **Ask why.** "I pay $15 for the tool I use now" is a ceiling and a competitor in one sentence.
- **Never price to the average.** If one group would pay $10 and another $60, a $35 price loses the first group and leaves money with the second. Two groups usually means two versions.
- **Watch behaviour over words.** A payment link, deposit or pre-order beats any survey. Stripe's payment links, on the studio's tool list, make that cheap.

Low prices are hard to raise and read as low quality. Sean Ellis and Morgan Brown, in *Hacking Growth*, advise testing a higher price before a lower one: buyers are usually less sensitive than founders fear.

## What to charge for.

How you charge often matters more than how much. The unit you price on should grow as the customer gets more value. Per user, per project, per thing produced, flat a month, or per result: pick the one that tracks what the customer actually gets.

- **Tiers do the sorting.** A good, better and best lineup lets people pick their own level. Each tier needs a one-line job (who it's for and why they'd pick it). A tier nobody can describe is where customers land by default and leave from.
- **Don't fence off what spreads the product.** Sharing, inviting a teammate or showing work to others drives growth. Charging for it chokes the thing that brings the next customer.
- **Free has a job, or it shouldn't exist.** Elena Verna's figures from her time at Amplitude are useful anchors: around 5% of free users ever pay, about 15% of a free trial with no card, and up to 70 to 80% of a trial that takes a card up front, though asking for the card scares many off at the door. Her reverse trial blends them: everyone starts with the full paid product for a set time, then drops to a free plan instead of being locked out.

For a solo product, free plans are expensive: every free user costs support and running time, and 5% conversion needs a lot of users. A short trial, or a paid plan with a refund, is usually the honest start.

## Retention is the base of growth.

Growth poured into a product people leave is a leaky bucket. Plot what share of each week's new users is still active week by week. A healthy line drops, then flattens. A line sliding to zero means more users would only mean more people leaving.

Superhuman's survey on the shelf is the quick read before the curve exists: ask active users how they'd feel if they could no longer use it, and look for 40% or more saying "very disappointed". Under that bar, fixing the product or narrowing who it's for beats any growth work.

And count the action that proves value happened, not signups or downloads.

## Traction: one or two channels, not ten.

Gabriel Weinberg and Justin Mares's *Traction* lists nineteen ways to reach customers, from search ads to sales calls and events. Founders over-pick the channels they already know. The fix, their Bullseye method, is a real idea for every channel, cheap tests of a few, then all effort on the one that works.

Brian Balfour adds the hard part. Growth tends to follow a power curve: companies that grow well usually get most of it from a single channel, and testing many at once usually finds none. At HubSpot, he writes, an incentivised referral loop plus Facebook ads made up 70% of one product's growth.

Two things make a channel fit:

- **The product must suit the channel.** Channels don't bend to products. Spreading by word of mouth needs value people feel in minutes. Search needs people who already type the problem into Google. You can't change Google; you can change the product.
- **The price must suit the channel.** Cheap channels like word of mouth, search and self-serve signup want cheap, low-friction prices. Expensive channels like sales calls need big contracts to pay for them. A price in the middle can be too dear for the cheap channels and too small for the expensive ones. Balfour calls this the danger zone, and his own example is a $25 tier at HubSpot that landed there and was swapped for $50.

For a side product, the honest early channel is often doing things by hand: emailing the ten people, posting where they gather, onboarding each one personally. ConvertKit's turnaround on the shelf was exactly that.

## Loops beat funnels.

A funnel is one pass: people come in at the top, some fall out at each step, a few pay at the bottom. Nothing at the bottom feeds the top, so growth is linear: more in, more out.

A loop feeds itself: a user does something that brings in the next user. Someone shares a document, the reader signs up to edit it, and some readers share their own. Or revenue funds ads that bring paying users who fund more ads.

The master asks "what does a new user produce that brings the next one?" If the answer is nothing, growth will always be paid for one user at a time. The funnel is still useful, but as a close-up of one step inside a loop, not as the whole model.

## Product-led growth.

Product-led growth means the product itself brings people in, gets them to the value, and asks for money, with little human help. Verna frames it as a question of when, not whether: product-led fits when the buyer is the user and contracts are small; sales earns its cost when deals are big or the product needs explaining. The day-job trap is calling every new signup as a sales lead before they've found the value. For a side product it's the default: the signup page, the first five minutes and the upgrade prompt are the whole sales team.

## Where it goes wrong.

- **Building first, pricing last.** Fix: ask a real price before the build, and let the answers shape what gets built.
- **One price for everyone.** Fix: look for groups that value it differently, and give them different versions.
- **Pricing too low out of nerves.** Fix: start higher, test upward, and cut only for a stated reason.
- **Cutting price when sales are slow.** A cut early on reads as an admission and is hard to take back. Fix: check first whether people understand the value, and whether a competitor moved.
- **A free plan with no job.** Fix: say what free is for, and count its cost.
- **Ten channels at once.** Fix: test a few cheaply, keep the one that works, drop the "kind of works" ones.
- **Growth before retention.** Fix: check the retention curve flattens first.

## Examples.

- **Cohen's ten who'll pay:** the price asked as a real number, before anything exists.
- **Buffer's two-page test:** a pricing page slotted in before the email box, testing demand and plan choice at once.
- **ConvertKit's commit or quit:** growth came back by narrowing the customer and selling by hand, one channel done well.
- **Superhuman's fit engine:** a retention check to pass before spending on growth.

## What a strong product leader would notice.

- No one has been asked a real price yet.
- The price was set by looking at competitors, not at customers.
- The free plan has no stated job.
- The growth plan lists more than three channels.
- The price sits between what cheap and expensive channels can carry.
- The headline number is signups, not people who came back.
- Nothing a user does brings in the next user.
