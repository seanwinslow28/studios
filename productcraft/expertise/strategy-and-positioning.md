# Strategy and positioning.

Read this when a job asks where a product should go, what it should stop doing, who it's for, or why anyone would pick it over what they use now. A good strategy is short and makes choices. Good positioning tells one kind of buyer, in their terms, why this beats their next best option.

## What a strategy is.

Richard Rumelt's *Good Strategy Bad Strategy* gives the cleanest test, and it holds for a side product as well as a company. A strategy has three parts:

- **A diagnosis.** What is actually going on, and what makes it hard. Not "growth is slow" but why it's slow.
- **A guiding choice.** The overall approach to that difficulty. It matters most for what it rules out.
- **Coherent actions.** Things people will actually do, each one making the others stronger, all serving the choice.

Most documents labelled strategy have none of the three: a vision, some values, a list of hopes. Rumelt's point is that the diagnosis is the hard part. Once the real obstacle is named plainly, the choice often becomes obvious. So read any draft by finding the sentence that names the obstacle. If there isn't one, there's nothing yet to judge.

## Telling strategy from goals and fluff.

Three look-alikes pass for strategy all the time.

- **Goals.** "Reach 1,000 paying users by June" is a wish, not a plan for getting there. A goal is fine as a target; it isn't a strategy until something says how the obstacle in the way will be beaten.
- **Fluff.** Big words that say nothing: "customer-centric", "best-in-class", "leverage synergies". The test: rewrite it in plain words and ask whether every rival could say the same. If they could, it isn't a choice.
- **A list with no spine.** Twelve priorities of equal weight. Rumelt's view is that good strategy picks one or a very few objectives and pours effort into them. A list that never says no is a budget, not a strategy.

Another tell is a plan everybody likes. Real choices disappoint someone, because something they wanted didn't make it. When a plan survives every meeting untouched, it's usually because it chose nothing.

## Lasting advantages.

A strategy is stronger when it rests on something rivals can't easily copy. Hamilton Helmer's *7 Powers* names the kinds of lasting advantage a business can hold, and asks two questions of each: what does it get you (higher prices, lower costs), and what stops a capable rival from taking it away?

His seven, in plain terms:

- **Scale economies.** Costs per customer fall as you grow, so a smaller rival can't match your price.
- **Network economies.** Each new user makes the product better for the others, as with a marketplace or a messaging app.
- **Counter-positioning.** A new way of doing business the leader won't copy because copying would damage its existing business. Vanguard's cheap index funds are his main example: the big fund firms lived on high fees.
- **Switching costs.** Leaving costs the customer time, data or money, so they stay even when a rival is a bit better.
- **Branding.** People pay more for the same thing because of years of trust in the name.
- **Cornered resource.** Exclusive access to something valuable, like a patent or a rare skill, on good terms.
- **Process power.** A way of working so built up and hard to see that rivals can't match it even when they study it.

Two of Helmer's points matter most for small products. First, most "advantages" in a pitch are just being good at the work, which is worth having but doesn't stop a rival. Always ask what the barrier is, not just what the benefit is. Second, a brand-new product can rarely hold most of the seven yet, because they need size or years. Early on, only two are realistic: counter-positioning, a model the big players can't follow without hurting themselves, and a cornered resource, something valuable you hold that others can't get.

## Positioning: who it's for, against what, why it wins.

Positioning is the context a product sits in, so the right buyer gets it at once. April Dunford's *Obviously Awesome* breaks it into five parts, and the order she works them in is the useful bit:

1. **Competitive alternatives.** What would the customer use if this didn't exist? Often it's a spreadsheet, a paid human, or doing nothing, not a rival app.
2. **What only you can do.** The abilities the alternatives lack.
3. **The value those abilities bring,** to the customer, in their terms.
4. **Who cares most about that value.** The best-fit customers, specific enough to find and list.
5. **The market category.** The frame that makes the value obvious and sets which products you're judged against.

Starting from the alternatives keeps it honest. Start from features and you describe what you built; start from the category and you promise value you can't deliver.

For Sean's side products the big lesson is narrowness. A small product wins by being clearly the best for a small group, not slightly better for everyone. If the honest answer to "who is it for?" is "anyone who...", it isn't positioned yet. Dunford's own check: if prospects keep asking how you compare with the market leader, you've accidentally placed yourself head to head with it, which a newcomer almost always loses.

## Cutting to focus.

The hardest strategic act is stopping things. Every product and feature that stays costs attention, support and clarity, even when it brings a little money in.

Good cuts share a few marks. They follow from the diagnosis, not from a percentage trimmed off every team. They're deep enough to hurt. And the work that remains fits together, so what stays is stronger for the cut. A cut spread thinly across everything is how a weak plan avoids choosing.

The common trap is keeping something because of what it cost to build. That cost is gone either way; the only question is where next month's attention does most good.

## Writing the announcement first.

Amazon's habit, described by Colin Bryar and Bill Carr in *Working Backwards*, is to write the launch press release before building anything, plus a page of hard questions and answers. The press release is under a page, written for the customer, and says what problem is solved and why it's better than today.

It's a strategy test in disguise. If the release can't name a customer, the problem, and why this beats their alternative in plain words, nothing is settled yet. Rewriting a page is cheap; rebuilding a product isn't.

## Where it goes wrong.

- **Goals dressed as strategy.** Fix: ask what's in the way and how the plan beats it. If the answer is "work harder", there's no strategy.
- **No diagnosis.** Fix: write the obstacle in one plain sentence before anything else.
- **A choice that rules nothing out.** Fix: list what the plan says no to. An empty list means no choice was made.
- **An "advantage" anyone could copy.** Fix: name the barrier. Being good at the work isn't one.
- **Positioning for everyone.** Fix: name the best-fit customers so specifically you could make a list of them, and their real alternative.
- **Comparing against a rival nobody actually meets.** Fix: find out what real customers used before, from them, not from a search.
- **Cuts spread evenly.** Fix: cut whole things that don't serve the choice, and keep whole things that do.

## Examples.

- **Apple's 1997 cuts** (`productcraft/research/apple-1997-cuts.md`): Rumelt's opening example. Diagnosis: nearly out of cash and making far too much. Choice: a tiny product line through few outlets. Actions: desktops cut from about 15 models to one, printers and add-ons dropped, most store chains dropped. Profit came back before the iMac shipped.
- **ConvertKit's "commit or quit"** (`productcraft/research/convertkit-commit-or-quit.md`): a solo maker's positioning fix. Revenue was sliding while the product was for anyone sending email. Narrowing to professional bloggers, then selling by hand, turned it round.
- **The Browser Company's letter to Arc members** (`productcraft/research/arc-letter-to-members.md`): cutting to focus with numbers in front, a middle path chosen on purpose, and an admission the call came a year late.
- **Cohen's "ten people who'll pay"** (`productcraft/research/cohen-ten-who-will-pay.md`): positioning in its earliest form. Before any strategy, find out who the customer is and what they do today.

## What a strong product leader would notice.

- Whether there's a sentence naming the real obstacle, or only goals.
- What the plan says no to, and whether anyone will be disappointed by it.
- Whether a claimed advantage has a barrier, or is just being good at the work.
- Who the product is for, named tightly enough to list.
- What the customer would use instead, from the customer's mouth.
- Whether the cuts are deep and whole, or thin and everywhere.
- Whether the one-page announcement could be written today.
