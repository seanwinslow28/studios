# Talking to customers.

Read this before any conversation with a possible customer, for a new idea, one of Sean's products or day-job work. A good conversation leaves you knowing something true about the person's life, in their words, with nothing you said steering it. Whether an idea is worth building is in the brief on that question.

## What a good conversation gets.

People are kind, and poor at predicting themselves. Ask whether they'd use something and most say yes, to be nice and because they picture who they'd like to be. That yes is nearly worthless and feels great, which makes it dangerous.

Rob Fitzpatrick's *The Mom Test* is named for this: even your mother would fib about your idea, out of love. His fix is to stop asking about the idea. Ask about their life and about things that already happened, and talk far less than they do. A good question is one even someone who loves you couldn't answer with a kind lie.

So the aim is facts, not approval. Jason Cohen's question to hold: what does this person know that would prove one of my beliefs wrong?

## Past behaviour over opinions.

The most useful habit is asking for one recent, specific story. Teresa Torres's opening asks about "the last time you...", with the blank scoped to what you need: the last time you chased an unpaid invoice, the last time you picked an app for this. A real story carries what a direct question never gets: when, where, who else, what went wrong, what they did about it.

- **"Usually", "always", "never" and "would" are warnings.** The person has slid from an event into a general claim. Let them finish, then steer back: "the last time it happened, what did you do?"
- **Test a guess without hinting at it.** Cohen's example: not "do you think of yourself as a blogger?" but how they describe their work to someone new. The first invites a yes; the second shows their real words.
- **Ask what they've spent.** Hours on the workaround, money on tools, someone they hired. Spending is caring, in the past tense.

"Will bookkeepers pay for this?" is a fine goal and a terrible question. You answer it by collecting stories, not by asking it.

## Don't pitch.

Once you describe your idea, the talk becomes a review of it and you collect manners instead of facts. Cohen says the commonest mistake he sees from the other side of the table is a founder who pitches for most of the call and leaves with nothing but polite approval.

Keep the idea out as long as you can. When it does come up:

- **Deflect compliments.** "I love it" tells you nothing. Thank them and ask how they handle the problem today.
- **Dig under feature requests.** "You should add X" is their guess at a solution. Ask what X would let them do and how they cope without it. Several people asking for one feature often want it for different reasons.
- **Deflect the market guru.** Most people eventually speak for others: "I wouldn't pay that, but lots would." They can't know. Treat it as their own view and bring them back to their own situation.
- **Follow strong feeling.** Anger, embarrassment and relief point at what matters. "Tell me more about that."
- **Walk it through.** If you do show something, ask them to narrate a real day using it: when they'd open it, what next, where the result goes. The missing piece that would stop the sale tends to surface there.

## Find the real problem.

A problem worth building for is one people already spend something on.

- **They've looked for a fix,** tried tools or built a workaround. Someone who complains but never searched won't search for yours. Cohen goes further: people who switched products or built workarounds mean it more than people who only complain, because complaining costs nothing.
- **It comes up unprompted.** Start broad, with what they're focused on or what's annoyed them lately, and zoom in only once they raise the area. Opening with "what's your biggest problem with X?" makes X a problem whether it is or not.
- **It's a need, not a solution in disguise.** Torres's check: could it be solved more than one way? "I want a faster horse" has one answer; "I'm always late to client meetings" has many.

If answers are still all over the place after ten or so conversations, the group is usually too broad, not the questions too weak. Narrow until you could name the place, online or off, where these people gather.

## Recruit the right people.

Talk to people who match the customer you're aiming at, not friends or whoever is handy. Friends say it sounds cool and can't tell you whether buyers would pay. Cohen found early WP Engine interviewees by searching LinkedIn for WordPress developers and asking for an hour: 40 of 50 agreed, and only one took him up on his offer to pay a fee.

With no product and no audience:

- **Ask for advice, not a survey.** People like being asked about their expertise; they dislike forms.
- **End every call asking for two more names.** Cohen suggests aiming at about 20 conversations; at one referral each, the supply never runs out.
- **Expect few replies.** Cohen calls 10% on cold messages fantastic.
- **Make it easy:** 20 minutes rather than an hour, a scheduling link, a clear reason it helps them.

Once a product exists, recruiting can run itself. A product manager Torres wrote about put an in-app pop-up offering $35 for a short interview, linked to a booking page, and had nearly a dozen booked overnight. About half didn't show, so book twice what you need. A rarer customer type took about a week to fill.

Paid recruiting through Respondent, about $40 to $80 a session plus the person's fee, is only for business buyers Sean can't reach for free.

## Keep a weekly habit.

Torres defines continuous discovery as the people who build the product talking with customers every week, in small ways, toward a clear goal. Weekly is the point. A big study once a quarter lands after the decisions it should have shaped. A conversation each week keeps every decision near a recent story, and makes a bad interview cheap, since another comes next week.

For Sean's side products, that means a small steady rhythm, not a burst before launch and silence after, with the person making the product doing the talking.

## From stories to a tree of opportunities.

Torres's opportunity solution tree puts the conversations to work: one outcome at the top, written as something customers do; below it, the needs heard in interviews; below a chosen need, several solutions; below each, small tests of what it assumes.

- **Only what you've heard.** A need goes on because it came up in real stories, not because the team imagines it.
- **Big needs above small ones.** "I can't keep track of what clients owe me" sits above "I forget the second reminder." The small one is something you can fix this month.
- **Compare neighbours.** Choose between needs that share a parent, pick one target, and set the other branches aside for now.
- **Several solutions per need.** Comparing three ideas teaches more than asking whether one is good.

## Synthesis that keeps the quote honest.

Write a one-page snapshot straight after each conversation: who they are, the story as it happened, the needs it surfaced, and one line in their exact words that will bring them back to mind months later. Keep your reading apart from what they said.

Quotes are where honesty slips. A tidied quote becomes a paraphrase, and a paraphrase becomes what you hoped they said.

- **Quotation marks only for exact words,** checked against the recording. Anything reworded loses them.
- **Label inferences.** "She seemed frustrated" is your reading; "I've given up on it" is a quote.
- **One person is a story, not a pattern.** Change a belief when several people say the same thing.
- **Separate piles for different customers.** One group answering one way and another the other is two segments, not noise.
- **Tools for the busywork.** Granola or parakeet for transcripts (parakeet for anything sensitive; the audio stays on the Mac), and the interview-summary skill for a first pass, quotes checked. Summarizing with Claude sends the transcript's text to Anthropic, so for day-job material ask Sean first or summarize with a local Ollama model. The thinking stays with Sean, which Cohen also urges.

## Where it goes wrong.

- **"Would you use this?"** Fix: ask for the last time it happened.
- **Pitching, then counting compliments.** Fix: no idea until the end, if at all.
- **Building the requested feature.** Fix: ask what it would let them do.
- **Interviewing friends.** Fix: people who match the customer, found by referral.
- **A burst of interviews, then nothing.** Fix: one a week, by the person building.
- **Needs on the tree nobody said.** Fix: only what came from real stories.
- **Quotes polished into hopes.** Fix: exact words, checked, inferences labelled.

## Examples.

On the shelf in `productcraft/research/`. **Cohen's ten who'll pay** holds his method: each guess beside the open question that tests it, and a real price at the end of calls that split his customers into two groups. **Superhuman's fit engine** shows whose voices to weigh: it set aside people who wouldn't miss the product and listened hardest to near-fans who valued its main benefit. **ConvertKit's commit or quit** shows Nathan Barry narrowing to professional bloggers and selling to them one by one.

## What a strong product leader would notice.

- Questions in the future tense: "would you", "will you".
- The interviewer talking more than the customer.
- A quote that reads too cleanly to be speech.
- A need on the list no customer voiced.
- Interviews only with friends, colleagues or the founder's own circle.
- Ten conversations with ten different answers, read as rich data.
- A live product with no interview in the last month.
