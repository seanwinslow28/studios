# Productcraft's front door

What this studio's front door adds to the shared front-door guide in craftwork's guides folder. The shared guide sets the method; this page says only what product work needs on top.

**Where the job lives.** Two places, never mixed, both named in `productcraft/folders.local`, which git ignores. If that file is missing, ask Sean for the paths.

- **Day-job work** goes in a folder named `<job>-<date>` inside the Productcraft folder of his private work repo. Its material never lands in this repo, in a personal job's folder, or anywhere public.
- **Personal ideas and startup sparks** go in a folder named `<job>-<date>` inside his Productcraft jobs folder beside the studios repo.

Ask which kind of job it is before anything else, because it decides where every file goes.

**The usual job, or a showcase.** For a personal product, ask next which of two jobs this is. Recommend the usual one unless Sean says he's building for the fun of it.

- **The usual job**, and the default: a call settled by real evidence. For a product idea, that means "would ten people buy this?", and the job ends in pursue, hold or scrap. Everything on this page is written for it.
- **A showcase:** Sean wants the product built for its own sake and for his portfolio, whether or not it would sell yet. 16BitFit is his example. Nobody is contacted. The ten-people test is still written down, then parked, and the product moves on to the other studios. "The showcase" below says what changes.

Day-job work is never a showcase: nothing from it goes in a portfolio.

**Explore first, or straight to the brainstorm.** When Sean brings no idea at all, recommend exploring first: the hunt below finds real pains, he picks one or two, and the brainstorm works on those. When he brings a spark, go straight to the brainstorm.

**The brainstorm.** A loose idea spark gets widened before it's judged: offer wide when the spark is open, quick when Sean already knows the problem. A day-job decision whose options are already named usually wants quick, and stops at the textbook answers if one of them is it.

## Explore first: the hunt

For when Sean arrives with no idea. The hunt looks for people who are already hurting, or soon will be, so the brainstorm starts from a real pain instead of a guess. It's the one piece of new research the front door does, and it hands back pains, never product ideas.

1. **A wide net or a niche.** One question, with a lean. A wide net searches across many kinds of people. A niche goes deep on one group: one Sean names, or one of three the lead offers from his notes and the taste page, each with a line on why.
2. **Four hunters at once,** one per kind of place. Each is a fresh helper that never sees the others' work, given the same aim and its part of `productcraft/tools/hunting-grounds.md`.
   - **Where people complain:** Reddit, X, YouTube, TikTok and Hacker News through `last30days`, and the trade forums, tool forums and blogs where that group talks.
   - **Where they already pay:** freelance job posts, paid templates and "hire someone to" posts. People paying today to make a problem go away are the closest thing to the ten who'd buy.
   - **Bad reviews of paid tools:** the one- to three-star reviews of software the group already pays for, read for what they wish it did.
   - **Pain they don't see yet:** routines people describe as normal ("every Monday I copy the orders into..."), job ads hiring people for repeat work, and changes coming that will hit a group that hasn't noticed: a new rule, a price jump, a tool shutting down. This is where Sean finds a chance to help someone who doesn't know they need it, and where "the world of tomorrow" (taste rule 6) lives.
3. **What each hunter hands back:** who hurts, in their own words, with a link and a date; how many distinct people, on how many sites; what they do or pay today; and which places it couldn't read. A place not read means "not heard", never "nobody".
4. **The lead merges.** A pain that turns up in two or more kinds of place ranks above one found in a single place, however loud. Paying beats complaining, first-hand beats hearsay, and a group someone could reach beats one nobody can. Count people, not upvotes.
5. **The hunt page.** A house review page with at most six pains, ranked, each with its evidence and the kinds of place it turned up in, and the lead's pick marked. Sean picks one, or two to brainstorm side by side. The brainstorm then runs on his pick, and the questions below follow.

Free first: the hunters use free sources, and `last30days` stays within its free calls; say roughly how many a hunt used. The hunt only reads. It never posts, joins, signs up, applies or asks anyone anything. A place behind Sean's own sign-in, such as a group he belongs to, is read only with his yes for that place.

## What the ask must pin down

In this order, one question each, until every answer is a named specific:

1. **The call, and what Sean will do with it.** The one decision this job ends in (pursue, hold or scrap; this option or that; go or wait) and what he'll do differently on each answer. If no answer would change what he does, it isn't a job yet.
2. **Whose problem, and what they do today.** A named group of people, never "users", and what they use, pay for or put up with now.
3. **What's already known, and Sean's guesses.** The evidence in hand: notes, call transcripts, numbers, earlier attempts. Then his guesses, written down as guesses, one per line. Look in the files before asking; ask only for what they can't tell you.
4. **The bar.** What evidence would settle the call, set before anything is looked at, with a number you can count and a date: "ten people who'll pay $20 a month by December 1". "If people seem interested" isn't a bar yet.
5. **The limits.** Sean's hours, money, the deadline, and any tool or route he named, which becomes a must-have.
6. **What this is not.** The bigger job it would be tempting to do (a full business plan, a roadmap, a launch) and the nearby questions left for another job, named so they're easy to rule out.

## The showcase

What changes when Sean picked a showcase at the start. The six steps and everything else run as written.

- **The ask.** The call becomes what the product is and what its first version does. What Sean does with it is take it to Devcraft for the build and, if he wants a film, to Motioncraft. The bar is still set, counted and dated, and marked parked: it says what would show that ten people would buy, and nobody is asked. "What this is not" always includes outreach, surveys, waitlists, interviews and payment links.
- **What good looks like.** Products like it, and the best case studies that show a product's thinking from first idea to finished thing: how the problem was found, the calls made and why, and what was cut.
- **The locks, in this order.** First, the guesses and the bar, with the test that would check them, written and parked so it's ready to run if Sean ever turns the product into the usual job. Second, the first version: who it's for, what it must do, and what's cut, sized for one person building with AI. Third, the shape of what he walks away with: the product plan, the case study's first chapter, and a brief for each studio next in line.
- **Evidence.** Public research only: the hunt's places, the shelf, the web. A gap stays marked as a gap; nobody is asked to fill it.
- **The finish.** The product plan; a short brief for the next studio's front door that answers that studio's own front-door questions as suggestions Sean can change, and says the job is a showcase; and the case study's first chapter. Then the scorecard. Sean carries the brief to the next studio himself.

**The case study** is the portfolio piece, and it grows as the work does. It lives in one folder, `case-study/`, inside the Productcraft job's folder. Productcraft writes the first chapter at its finish: how the idea was found, the calls Sean made with his reasons, what good looked like, and the parked ten-people test. Each studio that later works on the product adds its own chapter at its finish, with the real work shown. A chapter is made from the decisions record and review pages every job already keeps; nobody keeps a log for it. Publishing it is Sean's call, when and where he says.

## What good looks like here

- **The shelf first:** `productcraft/research/`. Jason Cohen's "ten people who'll pay" rule is Sean's pick and the current focus: guesses written down first, a bar you can count, and price asked with a real number.
- **Then where good product calls are shown with their numbers:** founders' own write-ups of how they tested an idea, public strategy memos and letters, the experiment write-ups of large testing programs, and the essays of product leaders who show their working.
- **Measure, don't admire:** the bar they set before looking, what counted as evidence (money, time or a signature, not compliments), how many people and how much money, how short the document that made the call was, and what they said they'd do if it failed.

## What the front door leaves to the job

- **No answer to the call.** Pursue, hold or scrap is decided on the evidence, at the end. The front door only names the call and the bar.
- **No test design, interview questions or survey.** Those are locked in step 3.
- **No new research** beyond reading what's already in hand, except the hunt when Sean explores first. No market sizing, no competitor sweep, no name, no price.
