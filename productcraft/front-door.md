# Productcraft's front door

What this studio's front door adds to the shared front-door guide in craftwork's guides folder. The shared guide sets the method; this page says only what product work needs on top.

**Where the job lives.** Two places, never mixed, both named in `productcraft/folders.local`, which git ignores. If that file is missing, ask Sean for the paths.

- **Day-job work** goes in a folder named `<job>-<date>` inside the Productcraft folder of his private work repo. Its material never lands in this repo, in a personal job's folder, or anywhere public.
- **Personal ideas and startup sparks** go in a folder named `<job>-<date>` inside his Productcraft jobs folder beside the studios repo.

Ask which kind of job it is before anything else, because it decides where every file goes.

**The brainstorm.** A loose idea spark gets widened before it's judged: offer wide when the spark is open, quick when Sean already knows the problem. A day-job decision whose options are already named usually wants quick, and stops at the textbook answers if one of them is it.

## What the ask must pin down

In this order, one question each, until every answer is a named specific:

1. **The call, and what Sean will do with it.** The one decision this job ends in (pursue, hold or scrap; this option or that; go or wait) and what he'll do differently on each answer. If no answer would change what he does, it isn't a job yet.
2. **Whose problem, and what they do today.** A named group of people, never "users", and what they use, pay for or put up with now.
3. **What's already known, and Sean's guesses.** The evidence in hand: notes, call transcripts, numbers, earlier attempts. Then his guesses, written down as guesses, one per line. Look in the files before asking; ask only for what they can't tell you.
4. **The bar.** What evidence would settle the call, set before anything is looked at, with a number you can count and a date: "ten people who'll pay $20 a month by December 1". "If people seem interested" isn't a bar yet.
5. **The limits.** Sean's hours, money, the deadline, and any tool or route he named, which becomes a must-have.
6. **What this is not.** The bigger job it would be tempting to do (a full business plan, a roadmap, a launch) and the nearby questions left for another job, named so they're easy to rule out.

## What good looks like here

- **The shelf first:** `productcraft/research/`. Jason Cohen's "ten people who'll pay" rule is Sean's pick and the current focus: guesses written down first, a bar you can count, and price asked with a real number.
- **Then where good product calls are shown with their numbers:** founders' own write-ups of how they tested an idea, public strategy memos and letters, the experiment write-ups of large testing programs, and the essays of product leaders who show their working.
- **Measure, don't admire:** the bar they set before looking, what counted as evidence (money, time or a signature, not compliments), how many people and how much money, how short the document that made the call was, and what they said they'd do if it failed.

## What the front door leaves to the job

- **No answer to the call.** Pursue, hold or scrap is decided on the evidence, at the end. The front door only names the call and the bar.
- **No test design, interview questions or survey.** Those are locked in step 3.
- **No new research** beyond reading what's already in hand. No market sizing, no competitor sweep, no name, no price.
