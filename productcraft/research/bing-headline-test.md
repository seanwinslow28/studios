# Bing's "too good to be true" ad headline test (2012)

## What it is

An honest write-up of a test that overturned a team's own priority call. An **A/B test** shows two versions to two random groups of users and measures which does better. Written up by Ronny Kohavi, who ran Microsoft's experiment group, and Stefan Thomke in *Harvard Business Review*, September 2017. The same story opens Kohavi, Tang and Xu's book *Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments* (2020).

Link: https://hbr.org/2017/09/the-surprising-power-of-online-experiments (full text may need a free login)

**Best part to look at:** the "revenue too high" alert, and the decision to check for a bug before cheering.

## How it turned out

- **A 12% rise in revenue, worth over $100 million a year** in the US alone, with no harm to the user-experience measures. Checked against the article on November 12, 2026.
- **The idea had sat ranked low for more than six months.** It took a few days to build; an engineer tried it because it was cheap.
- **Why it's believable:** the people who ran it wrote it up, the book says it was repeated several times over a long period, and the 12% matches across the article, the book and Kohavi's talks. The dollar figure varies a little ($100 million in print, $120 million in one talk).

## What makes it good, measured

- **One change, one main number,** with guard numbers on user experience so a revenue win couldn't hide a worse product.
- **Suspicion first.** Within hours an alert fired: revenue was too high, which usually means a bug. They checked before believing it.
- **Repeated, not trusted from one run.**
- **It admits the planning was wrong.** Kohavi's wider finding: only about one in three ideas tested at Microsoft improved the number it was meant to.

### What a weaker version would have done

Celebrated the 12% on day one and shipped it, or never tested it because the planning meeting ranked it low. Either way the team learns nothing about its own judgement.

### Why it's here

It shows reading numbers honestly: a fair test, a guard against fooling yourself, and humility about the team's own guesses. It's the model for any day-job call that leans on data.
