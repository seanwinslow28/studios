# Jason Cohen's "ten people who'll pay" rule and his interview method (2010, 2022)

## What it is

Two pieces by Jason Cohen, who later founded WP Engine, a large WordPress hosting company:

- "Yes, but who said they'd actually BUY the damn thing?" (August 9, 2010): the rule. https://longform.asmartbear.com/customer-validation/
- "The Iterative-Hypothesis customer development method" (September 17, 2022): how to run the interviews. https://longform.asmartbear.com/customer-development/

**Best part to look at:** the 2022 piece's example table, each guess beside the question that tests it.

## How it turned out

- **He used it on WP Engine.** He says 30 people agreed to pay $50 a month before the company had a name, a website or a product. That claim is his own; no second source.
- **WP Engine grew into a large company,** widely reported.
- **Thin spot:** it's a method, so the evidence is its author's results and how widely it's taught, not a study.

## What makes it good, measured

- **A pass bar you can count: ten people who say they'll pay.** Compliments, likes and friends' opinions don't count. He notes almost none of the hundreds of pitches he saw at a local startup program had ten.
- **A fixed order:** goals, then guesses, then questions, then interviews, then update the guesses. His list has 11 goals (who the customer is, how they cope today, what they'd pay, what makes them buy now).
- **Guesses written down before the first interview,** one per row, each with one open question beside it. His own 2009 guesses for WP Engine were about half wrong, which is the point.
- **Clear "don'ts":** no "would you buy this if we built it?", no leading questions, no pitching, no asking them what to build.
- **Price comes up with a real number.** Stop when interviews stop surprising you; three is too few.

### What a weaker version would have done

Shown people a mock-up, asked "would you use this?", counted every polite yes as proof, and started building.
