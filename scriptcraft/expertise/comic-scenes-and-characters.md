# Comic scenes and characters.

Read this when a script has people who need to be funny: building the cast, finding what a scene is about, or fixing a scene that has jokes but gets no laughs.

## What good looks like.

### Someone ordinary, out of their depth, still hoping.

A comic scene runs on a simple formula, which Steve Kaplan calls the comic equation. An ordinary person takes on a problem too big for them, without most of what they'd need to beat it, and keeps trying anyway. Remove any part and it drifts toward drama. Hope matters most: while the character still thinks this might work, we can laugh at how badly it's going.

Bob Belcher in "Best Burger" fits. He enters a burger contest without his key ingredient, which his kids are racing across town to fetch, and he keeps cooking. He loses the trophy. It stays funny because he never stops trying, and lands because the win comes from elsewhere: his son, and a full restaurant afterwards.

So hope is a dial. Turn it down on purpose for the dark beat, as Sean's best scripts do when a joke and a grief share one line, then decide whether it comes back.

### Everyone is trying to win.

Before asking what's funny, ask what each character wants right now, and let them go after it the quickest way their nature permits, however foolish it looks. The laugh lives in the distance between what they want and how they try to get it, not in a witty line.

A comic character believes every move will work; Kaplan calls this positive action. The *Always Sunny* gang are the purest case: each scheme launched in total confidence, cruel only when cruelty helps them win. Larry David in *Curb* marches a petty grievance straight at the people who'll punish him for it; as Sean puts it, Larry just lets out what most of us feel inside.

The character causes their own disaster: when bad luck simply lands on them they're a victim, and a victim is as unfunny as a hero. And when a joke only works if a character acts against their nature, cut the joke.

### The non-hero: short on skills, shortest on knowing.

The comic lead is a non-hero: someone without the skills the job needs, who has a go regardless. The fewer skills, the bigger the laugh, and knowing matters most. A non-hero runs a beat behind: they look twice, misread the room, and slowly find out what we saw a moment ago. A ticking clock stops them closing the gap.

Some non-heroes know they're sinking, like Bob. Others are sure of exactly the skills they lack. Michael Scott in *The Office* is the model idiot boss, with staff who must obey him anyway. His confidence is the engine: the less he understands a situation, the faster he takes charge of it.

Skills work as a dial across a piece. Add awareness or sincerity for one moving beat; take it away to get the laugh back.

### A world view, and the relationship underneath.

"The nervous one" is a type, and a type can do one thing. A world view can do anything. Write how the character sees the world and they'll act to fix it: a man who treats every room as a contest will try to win the elevator ride. People would also sooner be proved right than be happy, so when the disaster they predicted arrives, they feel vindicated first. In *Arrested Development* each Bluth sees a crisis through their own lens, so one event yields a string of wrong reactions, each in character. Characters also differ in how much they see, from oblivious to seeing everything and powerless; write each line from that spot.

A separate tool, the metaphorical relationship, looks for the relationship hiding under the surface one: two coworkers who are really a divorcing couple fighting over the stapler; a boss and staff who are really a toddler and worn-out parents. Everyone has lived inside the hidden one, so its behavior comes ready-made: the sigh when the other coworker "forgets" to refill the coffee machine is a marriage's sigh. The surface holds. They still talk like coworkers; only what they do gives the secret away.

### The blind one and the one who sees.

The laugh usually lives in the face of someone watching nonsense. In every beat, someone can't see the problem or is busy making it: the blind one. Another sees it, struggles and can't fix it: the one who sees, who stands in for us. Kaplan calls these the straight line and the wavy line. The names cross over with the old double act, whose "straight man" is the one who sees, so this brief uses the plain names.

This is focus, not casting: it moves from beat to beat, and only one character holds it at once. Bob usually sees while Linda and the kids commit to something ridiculous. In *The Office*, Jim's glance into the documentary camera is the whole reaction.

Write very little for the one who sees: an honest "What?" beats a quip, because wit is a skill and skills end the struggle. Keep everyone else blind; once a second character notices, the scene ends. Escalate by giving them more to look at, a new person, object or demand each beat. And write the reaction a real person would have, not a bigger one (Kaplan's active emotion). Sean's *Petey Possum* draws it: a mascot's grin that never changes is the blind one.

### One lie, and a small cast that earns its place.

A comic premise grants one impossible or unlikely thing and asks honestly what happens next. Play every consequence straight. Bolt on a second impossible thing and the two fight over the same few minutes; keep the stronger.

Bring a character on only because the story needs them or its question uses them, and keep the circle small. Even the waiter has somewhere to be: give every minor character a want of their own, so their reactions come from it, not from what the scene needs. Each should pay its way twice, in laughs and in plot. Archetypes, the stock roles of the oldest comic troupes (schemer, innocent, braggart), give a cast its setup at once; a cartoon family is one such troupe. One specific absurd detail per character, which Sean's scripts are good at, keeps a type from going stock.

### The reset: characters who never learn.

Sean's favorite shows never let their people change. The *Always Sunny* gang go through things that should change anyone and end up back at the bar as if nothing happened. The laugh depends on the flaw surviving; a character who learns the lesson has gained a skill, and skills cost laughs.

A short can reset on its last beat. *Don't Hug Me I'm Scared* puts nearly all its words before the horror, and it ends on one straight-faced line that leaves its puppets no wiser. When a piece does want change, keep it small and show it in what the character does. *The Bear*'s pilot is the far end of the dial: the same kitchen pressure with the laughs turned down, its grief carried by a can of tomatoes thrown away, never a speech.

## How it goes wrong, and the fix.

- **Characters perform jokes instead of wanting something.** Every line is funny and the scene has no spine. Ask what each one wants this beat.
- **The lead is a victim.** Bad things happen to them. Find the mistake they make, usually from vanity or nerves, that sets it off.
- **Everyone is smart.** Both people in the scene see everything and swap quips. Make one blind; cut what the lead couldn't know.
- **The one who sees gets the jokes.** Give them a look, a "What?", a hold. Sean's films prove it: his laughs often sit in a blank face held too long.
- **A crowd, not a cast.** His *Lord of the Flies* pilot names seventeen characters. Keep those the story needs or its question uses; merge the rest.
- **Shock or a spoken lesson does the character's work.** See the jokes brief and the dialogue brief.
- **Hope runs out by accident.** The scene turns heavy. Restore the missing part of the equation, or place the dark beat on purpose.
- **A gag breaks character.** A running joke stops landing because its owner has changed. *Arrested Development*'s pay off on rewatch because nobody does.

## Examples.

- ***Bob's Burgers*, "Best Burger" (2014).** Bob as the one who sees, and a lead who loses the trophy and still wins.
- ***Don't Hug Me I'm Scared*, episode 1 (2011).** A cheerful host who is the blind one, and a straight-faced reset after the horror.
- ***It's Always Sunny in Philadelphia* and *Curb Your Enthusiasm*.** Selfish wants chased in total confidence, and nobody learning.
- ***Arrested Development*.** One crisis, a family of different wrong reactions.
- ***The Office* (US).** The idiot boss, the staff who must obey, the look into the lens.
- ***The Bear*, "System" (2022).** The dial turned toward drama; it won the Emmy for comedy writing.
- **Sean's *Petey Possum* (2024).** A grin that can't react, against everything.
