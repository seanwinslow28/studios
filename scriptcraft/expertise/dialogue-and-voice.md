# Dialogue and voice.

Read this when people talk in a script, or a narrator does: drafting lines, giving each character a sound of their own, or fixing a scene where everyone sounds alike, says too much, or says exactly what they mean.

## What good looks like.

### Every line is someone trying to get something.

People talk to get what they want: to be liked, let off the hook, left alone, obeyed. A line is a move against whoever is in the way, not a way to hand the audience a fact. Ask what the speaker wants from the other person right now. If the answer is "to tell the audience something", the writer is talking.

Comic characters also expect every line to work. They think the excuse will land, the pitch will close, the speech will win the room. In Sean's *Intervention*, a drug dealer runs an intervention and can't stop steering the blame away from himself. Every deflection is an honest attempt to win, and that's why it's funny.

When a fact has to come out, put it inside a fight, where someone wants to say it and someone doesn't want to hear it. Better still, move it to a scene where somebody hears it for the first time, or let a prop or a sign on the wall say it in a glance.

### Say one thing, mean another.

Subtext is the distance between the words and the want. People present the self they want others to see, and the truth slips out when the words meet an action that contradicts them. How much a character hides depends on how safe they feel in the room. Alone with a friend, they say it straight. Facing a boss, a rival or a date, they talk about the weather.

Comedy pushes the mask to the limit. *The Office* runs on it: Michael Scott talks like the beloved leader he wants to be while every face in the room says otherwise. Dry delivery is the mask holding perfectly calm while the action gives it away.

A whole film can hang on the gap between what's said and what's happening. *The Big Snit* has about 280 words, many of them a newscaster's. A couple bicker over Scrabble while a nuclear war breaks out that we only hear, and the joke is how small their words stay.

### Each character sounds like nobody else.

A voice is built from choices: how long the sentences run, which words, what rhythm, what grammar, what the character would never say. Change one and you have a different person. The test is to cover the names; you should still know who's speaking.

The sharpest contrast is rhythm. The blind one, the character who can't see the problem, talks in run-ons that interrupt themselves, piling up "if"s and "but actually"s. The one who sees the nonsense answers in a word or two: short, plain, honest, the question anyone in the audience would ask. Their rhythms can't mesh, and the friction is the scene.

In *Bob's Burgers* every family member has a sound: Tina's flat monotone, Linda's sing-song enthusiasm, Bob's weary muttering. Sean's *Go Fish, Bob* spec catches those voices closely.

A stammer or a false start belongs to one character and tells us something. A man who apologizes for existing apologizes for a greeting. A teenager trying to sound like the version of herself she's seen on screen, and failing, is a whole character in three words. In animation a written "um" tells the voice actor the hesitation is the performance.

### Two shapes Sean's scripts keep returning to.

- **The cave-in.** A character refuses something on principle and folds on the very next line. In *Nothing But a Good Time*, a man turns down an offer, watches someone else enjoy it, and caves at once. The stand has to be sincere for the fold to land.
- **The flat reply as the button.** The button is the last laugh that ends a scene. In Sean's version, someone at the edge of the scene punctures the big moment with one dry correction or a return to business.

### The whole script still sounds like Sean.

Characters can sound wildly different while the script still sounds like one writer. That voice lives in the choices around the lines: which details get in, where the funniest word sits (usually last), what goes unsaid.

Sean's comedy is concrete and physical: named places, props, food, timing, not literary phrasing or showy metaphors. When a plain word and a fancy word mean the same thing, the plain one is funnier. Profanity, where it fits, goes in the way people actually talk, as rhythm, not sprinkled on for shock. For a brand or all-ages piece, turn the grit down by swapping words for milder ones that keep the bite, never by cutting the joke.

Puns have their home in the jokes brief: they belong to a character or a sign, never to the writer's own voice.

A narrator, when there is one, needs a point of view of their own. *Arrested Development*'s narrator calmly contradicts characters who are fooling themselves, so the narration is a joke, not a summary. Sean's Camus short, *I Quit* and *New Year's Resolutions* are all narrator-led, and the Camus narration got shorter and colder on its way to screen. A narrator who only describes what we can see leaves the audience nothing to work out.

### Dry beats loud, and less beats more.

Dry delivery under-plays what's happening. Dollar Shave Club's launch video sets up an expected yes in its first seconds and answers it with a flat no, then keeps pitching razors with a toddler shaving and a man in a bear suit in the picture. The words never describe the chaos behind them, and that gap is the joke.

The same goes for reactions: met with something terrible, a polite or practical reply beats a scream, because the audience supplies what the character skips.

Sean's dialogue barely changes from page to film; what his shorts add is stillness around it, and the writing-for-animation brief covers how to put those holds on the page.

Animation is pictures first, so a line has to earn its place against a look, a gesture or a pause. *Rejected* runs eight and a half minutes on about 123 spoken words. The exception: *Thunder Road* is about 1,500 words because the talking is the action, a eulogy collapsing in real time, and each of its eight apologies is a fresh attempt to recover.

### Show the change instead of saying it.

The commonest way dialogue sinks a short is a character announcing what they've learned. A change you hear about is a change you didn't see, and self-knowledge spoken aloud sounds like the writer stepping in front of the actor.

Sean's own scripts show the habit. In *Food Traveler* the hero's change arrives in a phone speech, and in his *Lord of the Flies* pilot a character states the moral. The fix in both is the same: put the change in something the character does, at the table, in front of the people it costs.

A sincere speech can still run long. What makes it work is that the character is trying to win something with it, and the world answers it a scene later, often badly. Only the writer's moral has to go.

## How it goes wrong, and the fix.

- **Everyone says what they mean.** Ask how safe the speaker feels and mask more, have them talk about something else, or pair the line with an action that contradicts it.
- **The lesson is said out loud.** Cut the speech and find the action that proves the change.
- **Everyone hesitates the same way.** In Sean's long scripts roughly one line in three or four breaks off or stammers, and the characters blur. Count the broken lines, give the stammer to one character, and give everyone else a different rhythm.
- **Ping-pong wit.** Two characters trade witty lines, each aware of the other, and nobody struggles. Let them talk past each other: the blind one chases his own track, the one who sees says almost nothing.
- **Every question gets a straight answer.** Have the reply dodge, deflect, under-react or answer a different question.
- **The joke is shouted or explained.** Cut the explaining line; let a still face or a silence do the work.
- **A line after the last line.** Stop on the strongest line or the picture after it; the story brief covers endings.

## Examples.

- ***Go Fish, Bob* (Sean's spec) and *Bob's Burgers*.** Each family member has a sound you'd know with the names covered.
- ***Intervention* (Sean's short), around the one-minute mark.** A held blank face, then the deflecting line.
- ***Break Time* (Sean's short).** The dialogue is nearly word for word from the *Fetish* script; the 20-second silent hold on Sam is where it gets funnier.
- ***The Big Snit*.** About 280 words: petty Scrabble talk against a newscaster announcing the end of the world.
- **Dollar Shave Club's launch video, the first 10 seconds.** The expected yes gets a flat no; a deadpan pitch over chaos.
