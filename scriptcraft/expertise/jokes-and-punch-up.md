# Jokes and punch-up.

Read this when a line has to get a laugh: writing the jokes into a draft, filling a slot that still says "joke here", or going back over a finished draft to make it funnier.

## What good looks like.

### A joke sets up a pattern, then breaks it.

Every joke does two jobs: it gives the audience enough to expect something, then gives them something else that still makes sense. A surprise they can make sense of in half a second gets the laugh. Elliott Kalan's point about the famous list of three is that it is simply the shortest way to set up a pattern and break it; a pattern can run longer when the joke needs it.

The best jokes stop one step short. The audience gets the start and the end and fills in the middle themselves, and that small act of working it out is the laugh. Sean's finished shorts do this with lists: they run the ordinary items past quickly, then hold on the item that turns.

### Every joke is saying something.

Under every joke sits a plain, unfunny sentence: the point. The premise is the funny way of getting there: a scenario, a comparison, a reversal, a "what if?" Old Spice's "The Man Your Man Could Smell Like" never names a feature; it lets an impossibly smooth man talk to the viewer while the bathroom turns into a boat around him, and the viewer works out the point alone.

Two checks keep a premise honest. Can you say its point in one plain sentence? If not, it is a smart idea, not a joke yet. And is it too clear, leaving the audience nothing to work out? Pick the least obvious premise that still lands. In a launch film the point is the product's one benefit; the launch-film brief covers how every joke carries it.

### Every joke belongs to someone.

The same point makes a different joke in a different mouth, so ask who is telling it before you write it. A character's joke has to sound like something only they would say.

Puns show this best. Sean loves *Bob's Burgers* for its dry, pun-filled jokes. Its puns land because they belong somewhere: a sign, a menu board, a character who would make that joke. Nobody on screen oversells them. A pun belongs to a character or a sign, never to the writer's own narrating voice; told straight by a narrator, it reads as cheese. *The Great British Baking Nightmare* takes a baking-show catchphrase literally and turns it into a picture.

### The exact word, then stop.

Choose the specific word over the general one: a named brand, an exact number, the right verb. Specific is funnier, and it is easier to write, because one real detail suggests the next. Sean's best scripts give each character one absurd detail that tells you who they are.

When a joke borrows a familiar way of talking, like an ad, a coach or a 1950s school film, copy it exactly. The audience has to recognize it without effort before the break can land. Then say it as plainly and briefly as you can; every spare word delays the turn. Put the funny word last, so nothing after it softens the hit. Stop rewriting once every word has a job; fiddling with one joke is often a way of avoiding the next.

### Building a run of jokes.

A run of jokes climbs on one scale: bigger, ruder, pettier. Each step should jump further than the last, and the run should stop at the top. Steps out of order fall flat, and so do steps on different scales. Two other shapes help. Drop suddenly into something harmless and new, and the fall is the joke. Or repeat the exact same thing when the audience expects a change, and the laugh grows as they realize nothing will change. That suits a world that resets, like the gang in *It's Always Sunny in Philadelphia* back at the bar. How a whole sketch climbs is covered in the sketch brief.

### Shock, or logic.

A dark turn is as funny as the logic behind it. Shock alone gets one gasp; a specific, logical joke keeps its laugh on a rewatch. Sean's own scripts show the difference: his shock beats are weaker than his jokes where the awful thing follows from the setup. His Camus short shows it. A cheerful 1950s list of life paths ends on a grim one, delivered with the same grin, and the school-film format makes it feel inevitable.

### Jokes that reward a rewatch.

Sean prizes *Arrested Development* and *Community* because the second viewing finds jokes the first one missed. That comes from layers, not from piling more punchlines into the foreground. Four layers do most of the work:

- **Background gags.** Signs, posters, props and labels the characters never mention. *Too Many Cooks* puts its killer in the background at about 20 seconds, long before anyone notices him.
- **Callbacks.** An earlier joke returns in a new situation and means something new.
- **Double meanings.** A line works in the moment, then means more once you know the ending. The narrator of *Arrested Development* often contradicts the line a character has just said.
- **Runners.** One bit returns every few scenes, a little changed each time.

Density has a limit: if every line in front is a punchline, nothing has room to land. Keep the main jokes clear and push the extras into the frame and into callbacks. Sean's finished shorts already do this: their posters, mugs and shop signs are some of his best rewatch jokes, and none were in the scripts. Write two or three into each scene's description, so they are written in on purpose, not discovered in the edit.

### The punch-up pass over a whole draft.

A master reads the draft for the laughs, not the plot: where has nothing been funny for too long? Does every setup reach its payoff? Is every pattern clearly set up, and clearly broken? Can a line do two jobs at once, funny and revealing a character, or funny and selling?

Then come the cuts. Drop the weakest jokes so the strong ones land; a run of weak jokes wears the audience down before the good one arrives. Cut any line that explains a joke, or where a character says the premise out loud. Make the silences longer where a blank face does more than a word.

Then fill the gaps. A placeholder in a draft is a joke still owed. Look at what comes just before and after it, whose voice it needs, and how much the scene's feeling can take; that rules out most jokes. When a line just won't land, the fault usually sits in one place: the shape (no clear pattern to break), the point (nothing being said), the speaker (wrong mouth), or the words (too many, too vague). Add a second laugh on the same line only if one comes to you on a reread.

## How it goes wrong, and the fix.

- **The joke is explained.** A character reacts by saying why it's funny, or the setup gives it away early. Fix: cut the explanation and trust the audience to fill in the gap.
- **Shock instead of logic.** The dark turn is the only joke. Fix: find the logic that makes the awful thing inevitable, then let it be awful.
- **Wording too fancy to see through.** Fix: say it in plain words and see whether a joke is left.
- **A placeholder left in.** Sean's older drafts still hold placeholders where punchlines go, and in his finished shorts those gaps were filled in the edit, often with a gross picture. Fix: write the gag on the page, built from what sits either side of it.
- **A joke in the wrong mouth.** A narrator's pun, or a quip no character in the scene would make. Fix: give it to the character it belongs to, or to a sign.
- **A callback that just repeats.** Fix: bring it back changed, in a new situation, so it lands a new laugh.
- **Setups that never come back.** Fix: pay each one off or cut it; the story brief has more.
- **Extra endings after the button.** The button is the last laugh or picture that ends a piece; anything piled after it drains the laugh. Fix: end on the button. The story brief covers endings.

## Examples.

- ***Bob's Burgers*.** Where its puns sit, and how dry the delivery is. "Best Burger" is the episode on the shelf.
- ***Arrested Development*.** Callbacks, background payoffs and a narrator who contradicts the scene. Watch an episode twice.
- ***Community*.** More jokes than one viewing can catch, with the plot still clear.
- ***Too Many Cooks* (2014).** The killer in the background at about 20 seconds, long before the turn.
- **Sean's *How To: With Albert Camus*.** A quick, cheerful list that stops dead on its grim last item: a dark turn earned by its format.
- **Sean's *Break Time*.** Background gags the script never had, and a last picture that tops the last line.
