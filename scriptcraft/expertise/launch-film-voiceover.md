# Launch-film voiceover.

Read this when the job is a launch film, an ad or a product video with a voice over it: while the one idea is found, when the words are timed to the seconds, and whenever a draft sounds like a brochure read aloud.

## What good looks like.

### One idea, with a fight in it.

A launch film makes one point. Before any line is written, a master can finish "this is a film about…" in plain words, and the answer has a fight in it: the product against something. Dollar Shave Club's launch video is great razors for a dollar a month, against razors that cost too much. Old Spice's "The Man Your Man Could Smell Like" is the man on screen against the man on your couch. "Here's to the Crazy Ones" is people who change things against everyone who calls them crazy.

A real idea can be said in a customer's own words, in one breath. When it only exists as marketing language ("a premium solution for the modern man"), there is no film yet, just a slogan looking for one. The strongest also own the awkward truth: Dollar Shave Club was a name nobody knew, one founder in a warehouse, and the film makes that the joke.

### The words are only ever heard.

Voiceover is copy nobody reads, so good voiceover sounds like one person talking to one other person, never a speech to a crowd. Its sentences are short, mostly, with the odd one-word line and one longer sentence that builds, so the rhythm never settles into a drone. It never shouts on the page; an excited script sounds desperate. A finished voiceover is much shorter than its first draft: lines that look fine on paper go slack in the mouth.

Old Spice talks straight to the women who buy most men's body wash: one calm, confident man speaking to her alone. Sean's own Camus short works the same muscle, a narrator talking to one person in short, slightly cold lines.

### Timing words to seconds.

The rule of thumb is about 150 words a minute: roughly 35 words for 15 seconds, 75 for 30. It is a starting point, and the shelf shows the range a master uses:

- **"Crazy Ones":** about 100 words in 60 seconds, under 2 a second. Slow on purpose; the pauses carry it.
- **Dollar Shave Club:** roughly 250 words in 93 seconds, about 2.7 a second.
- **Old Spice:** about 108 words in 33 seconds, over 3 a second.

Pace is a tone decision. Heart wants room; talky comedy can run fast, but only when every line is a joke or a turn. The end card is part of the budget, not an afterthought: a logo and tagline take several seconds, so a 15-second spot really has under ten, room for one scene and no build. In a well-timed script the silences are written in as beats, and the count holds up in a table read (the script read aloud, one voice per part), where a deadpan reader runs slower than the rule.

### Picture and words split the work.

The voice never describes the picture, and never drifts so far from it that the viewer can't follow. One track completes the other, or plays against it.

Old Spice makes the picture obey the voice: bathroom, boat, oyster, tickets, diamonds, horse, about six changes in 33 seconds, all in one take. Dollar Shave Club goes the other way: a toddler shaving, a machete, a man in a bear suit, and the words never mention any of them. "Crazy Ones" runs a list of labels over the faces they fit, and the faces finish each sentence.

### The product arrives as the turn.

A feature list is the commonest way a launch film dies. The product earns its place by answering the want or wrong the film sets up, at the moment things change.

Dollar Shave Club is the cleanest case of a funny film that still sells. Michael Dubin's draft ran four pages; the comedy director Lucia Aniello cut it to 93 seconds and tied each joke to a sales point. The price, the blades and why you don't need ten of them each arrive as a gag, a laugh every four to six seconds.

"Crazy Ones" never names the product at all. The turn comes about two-thirds through, on "because they change things," and the film ends on a white logo and two words. The product is the turn because the minute has made you want to stand on that side. Anything the turn doesn't need goes to the super (the words shown on screen), the site or the box.

### The get, and humor as seasoning.

Each beat has a second or two of the viewer's patience. A line with nothing to work out lands flat; a line that has to be decoded loses them. The best lines leave a small gap the viewer closes on their own, like Dollar Shave Club's opening, where the yes everyone expects comes back as a flat no.

Interesting comes first; funny is a seasoning on top, and the wrong one for some brands. When it fits, Sean's favorite ads play like Adult Swim sketches: an unbothered voice making a real sales pitch while nonsense escalates around it, never a shout. Each line goes off on its last word. Old Spice saves its button, the last laugh that ends the piece, for after the sell: "I'm on a horse," a reveal that explains nothing.

Heart runs on the same rules, slower. "Crazy Ones" never tells you to feel inspired; it shows the faces and lets the pauses work. Sean says it tugs at the heart like the Dr. Seuss book *Oh, the Places You'll Go!* Describing a feeling kills it.

### The ending is the idea.

The ending drives the premise home, and audiences forgive a soft middle far more than a soft landing. A film whose closing line won't come usually has no premise yet. "Crazy Ones" closes on a line that works like a proof, then the tagline. Old Spice tops its escalation with one plain absurd statement. The tagline is the last thing found, once the film exists: short, bigger than the product, able to stand alone beside the logo.

Sean's finished shorts add one more move: the voice stops early and lets picture and music finish, the way his films top their last line with a picture. The story brief covers setup and payoff.

## How it goes wrong, and the fix.

- **A feature parade.** The film feels like a manual. Fix: name the one want or wrong, let the product answer it at the turn, and send the other features to the super or the site.
- **Brochure voice.** Lines that read well and sound dead. Fix: hear it spoken, keep only what survives, mix long sentences with short ones, and aim it at one person.
- **Too many words for the time.** The read races and the end card gets squeezed. Fix: count against about 150 a minute.
- **See-say.** The voice describes what's on screen. Fix: rewrite each line to complete or counter its picture.
- **An encoded idea.** People ask what it was for. Fix: say the selling idea plainly once and let the comedy sit around it.
- **Jokes that don't sell.** People quote the gag and can't name the product. Fix: tie each joke to a sales point, as the Dollar Shave Club cut did, or lose it.
- **Funny as the whole point.** Random and loud, with no truth under it. Fix: start from the product's real truth; play the weird dry and confident.
- **Everything saved for the reveal.** Twenty seconds of setup for one surprise, and nothing left to enjoy once it's known. Fix: pay off as you go, the way Dollar Shave Club lands a laugh every few seconds.
- **An ending that stops instead of tops.** Fix: find the closing line before the middle; one button, then the logo.
- **The boss narrates the values.** A statement of belief turns into a pitch. Steve Jobs recorded a "Crazy Ones" read and chose Richard Dreyfuss's instead. Fix: pick the voice the idea needs, not the one with the title.

## Examples.

- **Old Spice, "The Man Your Man Could Smell Like" (Wieden+Kennedy, 2010).** 33 seconds, one take, the set changing under the words. Look at how every sentence brings a new impossible picture, and the button after the sell.
- **Dollar Shave Club, "Our Blades Are F***ing Great" (2012).** 93 seconds, a founder's deadpan walk through his warehouse. Look at the first ten seconds, where the expected yes gets a flat no, and count how often a joke is also a sales point.
- **Apple, "Here's to the Crazy Ones" (Chiat/Day, 1997).** 60 seconds, about 100 words, no product named. Look at the turn two-thirds through and the pauses around it.
- **Sean's *How To: With Albert Camus* (written and shot in 2015, posted in 2016).** A cheerful 1950s school-film narrator over a dark subject. Look at the quick list that stops dead on its turn, and a narrator addressing a single viewer.
