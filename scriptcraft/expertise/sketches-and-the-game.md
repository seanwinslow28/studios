# Sketches and the game.

Read this when a job is a sketch, a cold open, or any short comic scene built on one funny idea: when the idea is found, while it is drafted, and when a draft is funny for a minute and then sags.

## What good looks like.

### One funny idea, played as a pattern.

A sketch is one funny idea, repeated and pushed. Improvisers call that idea the game. It is a way of behaving that keeps coming back, not a thing that happens once. Sean's sketch Drug Deal, filmed as *Intervention*, is set at a family intervention, but that isn't its game. The game is that the dealer who sold the drugs runs the rescue and won't own any part of the problem. Put that way, it travels: a landlord lecturing tenants about mold he never fixed.

So say the game as a short phrase with no place in it. If the phrase only tells you where the sketch is set, you have a location, not a joke. Events can happen, but each is a fresh place to replay the pattern. Once the draft starts asking "and then what happens?", it has become a story.

### A normal world first, played straight.

Before anything odd can show, the audience needs an ordinary world to see it against: who, where, doing what. This is the base reality. Build it fast, play it with total belief, and start in the middle of the action.

A familiar format can be the whole base reality. *Don't Hug Me I'm Scared* is a tidy kids' show about creativity. *Too Many Cooks* is a 1980s sitcom title sequence. Both play their format perfectly straight, which is why the strangeness reads. Get its rhythm, wording and look exactly right, then break it. A parody that only copies the format earns a nod, not a laugh.

The failure is everyone odd from line one. Improvisers call it crazy town: with nothing normal to measure against, nothing stands out.

### The first unusual thing, and the reply that picks the game.

Early, ideally by the third or fourth line, something breaks the expected pattern. Then the one who sees, the character who notices the nonsense, reacts as anyone real would. That reaction tells the audience what is odd, and picks which game the opening grows into.

Then the pattern sets. One odd move is a surprise, a second suggests a rule, a third confirms it. From there, stay inside that rule and bring in no second strange idea. *Intervention* lands its premise in two lines and plays that one premise all the way through.

### Heighten and explore: if this is true, what else is true?

Once the rule is set, ask what each line adds. A good one takes the pattern a step past anything before (**heightening**), or gives the blind one, the character who can't see the problem, a reason he truly believes (**exploring**), or shows the one who sees taking the hit honestly. A line that adds nothing to the pattern is padding.

Heightening alone turns into a list, and the audience gets ahead of it. The reasons are what make the steps rise. Give the blind one a single, confident way of seeing the world and let every step come from it. *Don't Hug Me I'm Scared* builds this way: the sketchbook's run of cheerful little put-downs, each bossier than the last, all from one calm idea of what counts as creative. Keep the reason plausible; never explain one absurd thing with a second.

The one who sees climbs too: polite at first, losing patience in the middle, furious near the end. He needs a reason to keep listening: a boss, a customer, a place where you can't make a scene.

On the page you can plan the ladder, which an improviser can't. Widen the gap at every rung, and keep the stakes small. A war with a barista beats a war with the government, because a small setting leaves room to climb. *Curb Your Enthusiasm* lives on this.

### How long a game can run.

Sean rated *Too Many Cooks* only "Ok" and prefers Monty Python. He called it a modern Python sketch that keeps the joke going until it's not funny, then funny again, "because it's just so drawn out and you don't know how to feel." Casper Kelly wanted it to run uncomfortably long, and at 11 minutes it does. It survives by changing, not just stretching: six genre shifts, a killer planted in the background at 20 seconds, the slasher turn at about 44%.

What his preference suggests: treat running long on purpose as a stunt, worth doing once and only with a new turn every so often. For most sketches, the better bet is to top the game and get out while it is still rising.

### How a written sketch ends.

Improvisers end a scene by cutting away on a big laugh. A writer has to write the button, the last laugh or picture that ends the piece, and it is the hardest line in the sketch. A good button lands the game's biggest step, or shows the game from an angle no one expected, and then stops. The ways that work:

- **The top.** The biggest heightening, saved for last. Nothing follows it.
- **The snap back.** Return to the base reality with a straight face. *Don't Hug Me I'm Scared* runs about 20 seconds of frantic horror at 81% of its runtime, then cuts straight back to the tidy table. Nearly all its words come before the horror, and it ends on one straight-faced line: the sketchbook calmly telling the puppets never to be creative again.
- **The turn on the player.** The rule turns on the one who started it, or the one who sees finally gives in and joins: a stand that folds, as the last move.
- **The first image, changed.** Something planted early returns to pay off the game. Sean's scripts are strongest at this.
- **The closing picture.** His finished shorts nearly always top the last line with a picture; *Break Time* ends on a photo its script never had. Write that picture into the script.
- **The step down.** After the peak, drop to something small and innocent; the calm is the laugh.
- **Breaking the frame.** Python often ended by refusing to end, cutting away or stopping the sketch from outside. That works only when the whole show is built for it.

What doesn't work: tying up a plot, a character learning something, a burst of violence because nothing else came, or carrying on past the best line with extra endings (the story brief covers those). *Too Many Cooks* finally ends its credits, and the "show" lasts seconds: the game paid off in one move.

### Cold opens and the title-card joke.

A cold open is a sketch squeezed into the moments before a show's titles. Sean rates *It's Always Sunny in Philadelphia*'s among the best ever: a quick intro, then a title card that is part of the joke. The shape is the Gang's usual world at speed, one small selfish idea, two or three steps up, then a cut to the card. The cut is the final step and the card is the button. Write it as part of the joke, never as a label; a card that repeats the scene is dead air.

## How it goes wrong, and the fix.

- **A setting, not a game.** The pitch names a place and some characters. Fix: say the funny pattern as a short phrase, without the setting, before drafting.
- **Crazy town.** Everyone is odd from the first line. Fix: a normal world, one odd thing, and someone who reacts truthfully.
- **A list.** Each step is just bigger, and the audience guesses the next. Fix: give the blind one a reason in his own logic, and widen the gaps between rungs.
- **A flat one who sees.** He reacts the same at the end as at the start. Fix: raise his temper in steps that match the game.
- **Winking.** Characters remark on how mad it all is. Fix: everyone believes their world; the audience does the noticing.
- **Story creep.** The middle chases what happens next. Fix: ask where else the same pattern could play.
- **No button.** The sketch trails off, or piles extra endings after a good one. Fix: end on the biggest step, often with a picture.
- **A gap where the joke goes.** Fix: write a rough gag now; the jokes brief covers the rest.

## Examples.

- ***Don't Hug Me I'm Scared*, episode 1 (2011).** Look at the hard cut from the gory montage to the tidy table.
- ***Too Many Cooks* (Adult Swim, 2014).** How long one idea can run. Look at the first two minutes: the killer is in the background before anyone notices.
- ***It's Always Sunny in Philadelphia*'s cold opens.** A sketch in miniature, with the title card as the button.
- **Sean's *Intervention*.** One premise, clear at once and played all the way through: the dealer who won't own his part.
- **Monty Python.** The yardstick Sean named for sketch: odd ideas played with total belief.
