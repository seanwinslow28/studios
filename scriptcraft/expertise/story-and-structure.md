# Story and structure.

Read this when a script needs its shape: when an idea first becomes a story, when a draft feels long or flat, when the ending won't land, and whenever a story has to fit a new length.

## What good looks like.

### Someone wants something, and something is in the way.

A story starts when a person wants something concrete and the world says no. The want needs an object you could point a camera at: get through the eulogy, win the contest, get her back. "Be happy" makes a hero who waits around. The best obstacles grow out of the character's own flaw.

Under the want sits a need: what the character lacks and won't admit. The want drives the plot; the need is what the story is about. In *The Bear*'s pilot, "System," the grief over a dead brother is never put into a speech. It lives in objects and routines, from the wordless dream that opens the episode to the silent last minute, when the chef throws out a can of tomatoes meant for his brother's recipe.

Comedy bends this. The *It's Always Sunny in Philadelphia* gang and Larry David glimpse what they need and turn it down; the snap back to type is the joke, and the reset is the format. Write the glimpse anyway: a refusal is only funny if we saw the door open.

### Every scene has the same shape as the whole.

A scene is the whole story in miniature. Two people pull in opposite directions, and the scene is over when the balance tips and something can't be taken back. If nothing is different at a scene's end, it is dead weight, however good the lines. Start once the pull is already under way, stop the moment it tips, and let the next scene inherit the mess.

Because the shape repeats, a whole short can be one scene stretched out. *Thunder Road* is thirteen minutes in a single take: a police officer gives his mother's eulogy. About half is rambling, with eight separate apologies. At 52% he announces her favorite song, the children's boombox fails, and from 59% to 80% he performs it himself with no music. That failure is the film's turn, and it is the move every scene makes: the plan hits something, and the answer is worse, and braver, than the plan.

It holds without fiction too: in Bourdain's *Parts Unknown* narration in Southern Italy, a food segment turns when the voice steps off the plate into a memory.

### The turn, and the climb to it.

The middle is where a story flips: the character acts like their opposite for the first time, or learns what changes the fight, and the stakes rise because of it. Each step of the climb should be the worst likely result of the one before, so it feels earned, not random. That is escalating absurdity until it tips over, in structural terms.

*Rejected* climbs down four steps in nine minutes, each marked by a title card, and the final collapse lands at 84%. *The Big Snit* runs two ladders side by side: a couple's Scrabble squabble gets pettier on screen while a nuclear war we only hear breaks out. The joke lives in the gap.

Pressure keeps a story moving, and comfort ends it: several critics said *Chef*'s conflict drains away once the food truck hits the road. *Bob's Burgers*' "Best Burger" keeps the pressure on by letting Bob lose the contest; the win comes from somewhere else.

### Beats on cards.

A beat is one change, in what a character does or feels. Give each its own card, so the whole piece sits in view at once and can be rearranged without rewriting a page. When a draft sags, spread the cards out and pick the handful you would fight to keep. Often the story is just those, and everything else was scaffolding.

The cards also show the shape, and the shelf has several. *Rejected* is one ladder down; *The Big Snit*, two ladders at once; *Thunder Road*, one scene stretched to a film. *Don't Hug Me I'm Scared* repeats a cheerful pattern and breaks it once: nearly all its words come before the horror, and it ends on one straight-faced line. (How a sketch snaps back is the sketches brief.)

### Plant early, pay off last.

What is planted early comes back late, ideally on the last line or the last picture. This is Sean's strongest move: every one of his scripts has one. *Resolutions* plants a small thing in its first shot that becomes its last joke. When the last frame answers the first, the ending feels inevitable.

The reverse is the set-up that never returns: a threat, a debt, a fear, planted and dropped. Each is a promise, and a broken one reads as a mistake even when nobody can say why. Sean's long scripts collect these; his short pieces mostly don't.

### Ending on the button.

The button is the last laugh or picture that closes a scene or a whole piece. Find the one that closes the loop the opening set up, and stop there. Two ways to miss it: stopping a beat after the best line, so the piece trails off, or piling extra endings after it, so each one spends some of the laugh.

Sean's finished shorts show both sides. *Break Time* ends on the same dry line as its script, and then, once the credits roll, a still picture tops it. *Petey Possum* has its cleanest ending in a long hold on its hero's frozen grin, then adds two more endings after it. On the page, his New Year's and Fetish scripts each run a beat past their best line.

### One shape, three lengths.

The shape compresses because it repeats. Take a made-up premise: a man who insists he can build a wardrobe without the instructions.

At ten minutes there is room for all of it: the flaw under the want (he can't ask for help), attempts that each make things worse, a middle where he opens the instructions and they make it worse still, a worst point, and a closing image that answers the opening.

At sixty seconds, as a launch film for a made-up assembly app, the change moves from the character to the viewer: the problem, the usual fix failing, the product as the turn near the middle, a taste of life after, the button. Dollar Shave Club's 93-second launch video is the shelf's model for this length.

At fifteen seconds, about 35 spoken words, the middle collapses into one beat: the man, the box, the turn, the button. The shelf has nothing that short; Old Spice's 33-second spot is the nearest. (Timing and wording are the launch-film brief.)

What survives every cut is the want, the turn and the payoff. What goes first is the middle.

## How it goes wrong, and the fix.

- **The turn stays off screen.** Sean's main habit: the decision or the set piece is reported or cut away from. Fix: put it on screen. The exception is when the gap is the joke: *The Big Snit* keeps the war off screen because the couple never notice it.
- **Planted things left lying around.** Fix: list everything planted, then pay each off or cut it.
- **A scene with no one in the way.** A character asks and gets; nothing turns. Fix: give the other side an opposite want, or cut the scene.
- **Comfort sets in.** Obstacles vanish and the story turns into a montage, as in *Chef*'s second half. Fix: keep the pressure on to the end; let someone else take the trophy.
- **The change is said, not shown.** Fix: let one action prove it, like *The Bear*'s can of tomatoes. (The spoken lesson itself is the dialogue brief.)
- **The ending trails off, or won't stop.** Fix: end on the button that pays off the premise, often a picture, and cut everything after it.
- **A slow start.** Sean's Camus short spends its first minute without a joke. Fix: cut from the front until the first beat holds a problem. *Oktapodi* sets up its lovers in about twenty seconds.
- **Too much story for the length.** A sixty-second spot carrying a character's growth and a feature list. Fix: count beats against seconds early.

## Examples.

- ***Rejected* (Don Hertzfeldt, 2000).** One ladder down. Look at the last minute and a half.
- ***The Big Snit* (Richard Condie, 1985).** Two ladders, the joke in the gap. Look at the cat knocking out the TV just before the war is announced.
- ***Thunder Road* (Jim Cummings, 2016).** A whole short as one scene, turned by a failed boombox. Look at 52% to 80%.
- ***Parts Unknown*, "Southern Italy" (Anthony Bourdain, 2017).** Narration with its own turn. Look at the sea-urchin pasta.
- ***The Bear*, "System" (2022), against *Chef* (2014).** Grief in objects, against a story that loses its pressure. Look at *The Bear*'s last minute and *Chef*'s first act.
- ***Oktapodi* (Gobelins students, 2007).** Two minutes, set up in twenty seconds, ending on a cruel last-second snatch.
- **Sean's *Break Time*.** A closing picture after the credits that tops the last line.
