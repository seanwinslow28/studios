# Writing for animation.

Read this when a script is headed for animation: a cartoon short, an animated sketch, a wordless piece, or any page that a storyboard artist will draw from.

## What good looks like.

### Write only what we can see and hear.

In animation nothing is on screen unless someone draws it, so every action line asks for a picture. Write what the audience sees and hears, in the order they see it: no thoughts, no backstory, no "she realizes." If a character is scared, write what scared looks like on this character: the ears flatten, the hand finds the doorframe.

One sentence, one picture, is a good rhythm; a new paragraph means a new shot or beat. Steer the camera by the order you name things, not with camera words. Start on a small detail and widen to the room, and the artist boards a close shot pulling out. Sean's *Petey Possum* script shows how far one line can go: a single wide shot of an empty bus tells a whole reversal, and nobody needs to explain it.

### Action lines are the performance.

In live action an actor brings the acting. In animation every blink is someone's decision, and the script makes it first. So write the verb with the attitude in it. Not "he walks to the desk" but "he edges to the desk like it might bite."

Write the gap between the words and the body: a cheerful line over a twitching eye is two jokes, and only the action line delivers the second. A face that can't react helps: Petey's grin never changes, whatever is done to him, which makes him the blind one, the character who can't see the problem. Building the performance shot by shot is in Motioncraft's character and acting brief.

### Writing the holds.

A hold is a stretch where the picture stops changing: a face that doesn't react, a room where nothing moves. It is one of animation's strongest jokes, and it is almost never on the page. Sean's finished shorts hold far longer than his scripts asked, and that's where they're strongest. In *Break Time* a character stands alone in silence for about twenty seconds. In *Resolutions* a spell fails because nothing on screen moves. When the drawing barely moves anyway, stillness itself gets the laugh.

Those holds were found in the edit. The next script should write them in: what we hold on, how long, and what breaks it. "Three seconds of nothing, then the reply" tells the artist and the editor exactly where the laugh lives. A hold that is only "Beat." gets cut to half a second by someone in a hurry.

Holds work best against speed. Sean's shorts run a list fast, one quick shot per item, then sit still on the item that turns. Write it that way: short lines, then the hold.

### Gags only animation can do.

Sean praised *Oktapodi* for doing what only animation does well, like *Looney Tunes*. Hold every visual gag to that: if it would play the same in live action, ask what the drawing could add.

What drawing adds:

- **Bodies that bend.** *Oktapodi*'s octopus stretches across a moving windscreen, gets flung off and slaps back on. Soft bodies squash, stretch and snap back, and the hit still has weight.
- **A world with its own physics.** A cartoon can run off a cliff and hang there until it looks down. Set the rule early and keep it, so breaking it can be the joke.
- **Hurt without harm.** Sean said *Don't Hug Me I'm Scared* stays funny because they're puppets. Drawn and stitched bodies give the distance that lets violence land as comedy, which is how *Petey Possum* gets away with a fountain of blood.
- **The medium itself.** In *Rejected* the paper crumples and tears, and the page swallows the last figure. Only a drawn film can destroy its own frame.
- **Ugly on purpose.** Sean likes how the ugly characters in *The Big Snit* and *Bob's Burgers* add to the comedy. Design is part of the joke, so describe it.

Write a physical gag as a chain of short clauses, each a beat the artist can stage: it hits the wall, bounces, lands in the soup.

### Scripts with no dialogue.

When the words go, the script becomes a list of visible changes, each showing what the character wants and what's in the way. *Oktapodi* has no dialogue: it sets up its lovers in about twenty seconds, yet its first shot holds for about thirteen of them, so we care before the chase.

The shelf shows a pattern: words run out as the pictures take over. *Rejected* has about 123 spoken words in eight and a half minutes, and its last minute and forty seconds are pictures alone. In *Don't Hug Me I'm Scared*, nearly all its words come before the horror, and it ends on one straight-faced line. *The Big Snit* goes nearly wordless from about 70 percent, into the aftermath. In all three the pictures take over at the end.

A wordless script leans on contrast between bodies (soft against bony, big against small), on sound written into the page, and on props that carry history and do the explaining. Imagine it muted: if a beat only works with sound or a caption, the picture hasn't done its job. Dialogue isn't forbidden; keep it short and in character, and see the dialogue brief for how it should sound.

### Timing a page.

An animated page plays shorter than a live-action one, about 40 seconds as a rough guide. Page count lies about action: "they fight" is one line and could run three seconds or thirty. A held silence takes no room on the page and real time on screen. The only honest timing comes from performing it, every line spoken and every action acted out, against a clock.

A short holds more than its length suggests when every shot has one job. *Oktapodi*, made to its school's cap of about two minutes, holds about 55 shots, around 2.2 seconds each, speeding up to about a cut a second in the van fight.

### Writing so a storyboard artist can see it.

A storyboard artist reads the script looking for pictures; give them pictures, and leave them room. Name the prop that matters and where it sits; skip the furniture that doesn't. Choose how close each shot sits by what it should show or hide: a wide shot that shows what the close-up hid is one of animation's cleanest punchlines. Give camera notes only where the shot itself is the joke. Background gags, the signs and props that reward a rewatch, are covered in the jokes brief.

End on a picture. The button, the last laugh that ends the piece, is usually best as an image: all but one of Sean's finished shorts top the script's last line with a sight gag. Write that closing image into the script. What happens after the script, from boards to the timed rough cut, is in Motioncraft's staging brief and its story-without-words brief.

## How it goes wrong, and the fix.

- **The novel on the page.** Thoughts and feelings no one can draw; a sentence an artist can't sketch. Fix: write the behavior that shows the feeling.
- **A placeholder where the gag should be.** "Something funny happens." An artist can't board "funny." Fix: write the gag in beats.
- **The live-action gag.** A joke that would play the same with real actors. Fix: push it toward what only drawing can do: the bend, the physics, the frame.
- **Lines that do the picture's job.** A character says what a face could show. Fix: cut the line and write the reaction.
- **Missing holds.** The script rushes past the silence that will be the laugh, or marks it only "Beat." Fix: write what we hold on, for how long, and what breaks it.
- **One long action paragraph.** Fix: one beat per line, so each gets its own drawing.
- **Rules that drift.** Gravity works in one scene and not the next. Fix: set the physics early; break them only as a joke.
- **Ending on a line, not an image.** Fix: write the closing picture. Extra endings after the button are the story brief's subject.
- **Too much story for the time.** The page count looked fine; the clock didn't. Fix: perform it, time it, cut.

## Examples.

- ***Oktapodi* (Gobelins students, 2007), 0:46 to 1:03.** The octopus fights the van driver across the windscreen: soft bodies, real weight, no words.
- ***Rejected* (Don Hertzfeldt, 2000), the last minute and a half.** The paper itself tears; words have run out on purpose.
- ***The Big Snit* (Richard Condie, 1985).** The cat knocks out the TV just before the husband sees the war announced: a gag that keeps the couple from ever knowing.
- ***Don't Hug Me I'm Scared*, episode 1 (2011).** A horror burst told purely in pictures inside a cheerful puppet format.
- ***Looney Tunes*.** Cartoon physics as a rule set, broken for the laugh.
- **Sean's *Break Time*, *Resolutions* and *Petey Possum*.** Long held silences, a face that never changes, and endings topped with a picture.
