# Scriptcraft's books, researched properly

## The simple version

Every pick below is on sale as an ebook today. Most of the old list stands: the three "no ebook" books all have one. One swap: Joke Farming replaces Poking a Dead Frog, because it teaches joke building. About $70 in all.

1. **Sketch: The UCB Comedy Improvisation Manual** (2023 edition). The deepest book on "the game". Kindle only, $25, as page pictures.
2. **Comic scenes: The Hidden Tools of Comedy** (Steve Kaplan). Characters who keep trying and losing. $12.
3. **Structure: Into the Woods** (John Yorke). Keep it; don't add Truby. $10.
4. **Jokes: Joke Farming** (Elliott Kalan). A step-by-step joke method. $5.
5. **Ads: Hey Whipple, Squeeze This**, sixth edition. $18.

Blazer's Animated Storytelling stays borrowed from Motioncraft.

**What you need to decide:** yes or changes. The close call is sketch: the UCB manual, or Anne Libera's Funnier (2025), which has flowing text but gives the game one section. My pick: the UCB manual.

## 1. Sketch comedy: finding the game and heightening it

**The pick: The Upright Citizens Brigade Comedy Improvisation Manual** (Matt Besser, Ian Roberts and Matt Walsh; second edition, January 2023).

Why it wins:

- It's the book the whole modern idea of "the game" comes from in teachable form: spot the first unusual thing in a scene, say what makes it funny, then play it again bigger, in new ways, until it tips over. That's exactly how Too Many Cooks and an Always Sunny cold open work.
- The whole book is about the game, not one chapter of it. It walks through two-person scenes first, then group pieces.
- Its weak spot is that it's written for improv performers, not sketch writers. The ideas carry over; most sketch writers in American comedy trained on them. The things it doesn't cover, like how a written sketch is laid out and how to end one, are covered by Funnier and by Kaplan.

**The catch:** the ebook is Kindle only. Google Play has only the audiobook, and Apple Books and ebooks.com don't carry it. The Kindle edition is a "Print Replica", meaning photographs of the printed pages rather than text that reflows. It reads fine, but turning it into notes means working from page pictures, as Motioncraft does with some of its books.

**Runners-up:**

- **Funnier: A New Theory for the Practice of Comedy** (Anne Libera, October 2025). Libera has directed at The Second City for about 30 years and founded a university degree in comedy writing. Stephen Colbert and Aidy Bryant endorse it, and four working TV comedy writers wrote the foreword. It covers jokes, characters, sketch (with a game-of-the-scene tool), sitcom stories and how to make something funnier, on top of a theory that comedy needs recognition, pain and distance. That theory suits your cheerful-surface, dark-turn taste. It loses to the UCB manual only because the game gets one section instead of a whole book. If you want flowing text in every store, take this instead. Kindle $27.54, Apple Books $29.99, Google Play $30.
- **How to Be the Greatest Improviser on Earth** (Will Hines, 2016). Hines taught at UCB for years and writes the clearest short pieces on the game anywhere. But the book is a loose collection of advice for performers, and it's Kindle only. His free newsletter posts on the game are a good free extra either way.
- **Truth in Comedy** (Charna Halpern, Del Close and Kim Johnson, 1994). Where the game idea started. It's about long-form improv rather than sketch, and much thinner on method. Kindle (2026 edition) and Apple Books $6.99.

**Ebook:** Kindle, $25.00, Print Replica (page pictures), copy-protected like every Kindle book.

## 2. Why a comic scene works: character, wanting, losing

**The pick: The Hidden Tools of Comedy** (Steve Kaplan, 2013).

Why it wins:

- It's the only book whose whole subject is this. Its core idea: comedy is an ordinary, flawed person going after something they can't get, without the skills to get it, who never gives up. That is Larry David, Michael Scott, the Always Sunny gang and the Bluths, almost word for word.
- It gives tools an agent can use line by line: characters try to win every moment; the comic lead is a "non-hero"; the straight character versus the wild one; positive action even while losing. It tests them on real film and TV scenes that work and scenes that don't.
- Kaplan created HBO's comedy workshop and New Writers Program and has run his workshop for Disney, HBO and DreamWorks Animation writers and animators.

**About Joke Farming here:** Sean asked whether Kalan's book could replace Kaplan. I looked hard, and it doesn't fit this area. Its unit is the joke, not the character or the scene: character comes in only through the joke-teller's voice, and narrative comedy gets one chapter of seven on uses. Kaplan, Vorhaus and Libera all teach comic characters better. But Joke Farming wins area 4, so it's on the list anyway.

**Runners-up:**

- **The Comic Toolbox** (John Vorhaus, 1994). A former sitcom writer's practical tools: the comic flaw, putting a character in the wrong world, comic opposites. It's assigned in a university TV comedy-writing course. Older examples. Kindle $9.99, Apple Books $9.99.
- **Funnier** (Libera, 2025), from area 1. Its chapter on comic characters covers this area well. If you want one book for areas 1 and 2, it's this one.
- **The Comic Hero's Journey** (Kaplan, 2018). A follow-up on story structure for comic heroes, not a replacement.

**Ebook:** Kindle $12.12. ebooks.com $27.95 (copy-protected EPUB). Not on Apple Books or Google Play.

## 3. Story structure at any length

**The pick: keep Into the Woods** (John Yorke, 2013; American edition 2014). Don't replace it with Truby, and don't add Truby for now.

**What each teaches:**

- **Yorke:** every story has one shape underneath: a flawed lead, a call, a trip into the unknown, a turn at the middle, a crisis, a choice, a changed return. He lays it out as five acts, then shows the same shape repeating inside every sequence and every scene, so each scene is a small story with its own turn. He explains *why* the shape works rather than handing out a template. He also argues a returning series character can't truly change, which gives the agent words for characters who never learn and reset, like Always Sunny's.
- **Truby:** stories should grow from the idea and the characters, not from a three-act template. Seven key steps (weakness, desire, opponent, plan, battle, realization, new balance), then a cast built as a web of contrasts where the best opponent attacks the hero's weakness, then theme argued through action, then a 22-step plot built last. Strong on worlds and symbols too.

**How they overlap:** most of the core is the same: wanting, flaw, opponent, a crisis choice, change, a new balance. Both reject rote templates, and both say a scene mirrors the whole story.

**Which serves short comic films better: Yorke.**

- A two-minute short or a sketch is one sequence long. Yorke's "same shape at every size" applies to it directly. He has even applied his five acts to Christmas TV ads.
- He's shorter and gives the agent one model.
- Truby's 22 steps, symbol web and scene weave are built for features and novels, and he insists the hero must change and see the truth. That fights the comedies you love, where nobody learns anything.

Truby would earn a place later if Scriptcraft starts writing pieces over ten minutes or with big casts. For designing a cast, his character web is the best tool on any of these shelves.

**Runners-up:**

- **The Anatomy of Story** (John Truby, 2007). See above. Apple Books $12.99, Google Play $12.99, ebooks.com $12.99.
- **Invisible Ink** (Brian McDonald; revised 2024). Short, built around one theme sentence, by a story teacher Pixar brought in for its staff. Kindle only, and the Pixar reading-list claim is second-hand.
- **Writing Short Films** (Linda Cowgill, 2005). The best-known textbook on short scripts, but readers say it's mostly general structure, and it's dated. Apple Books $14.99.

**Ebook:** Kindle $9.99, Apple Books $9.99, Google Play $9.99 on sale ($19.99 list).

## 4. Joke writing and punch-up

**The pick: Joke Farming: How to Write Comedy and Other Nonsense** (Elliott Kalan, University of Chicago Press, November 2025). It replaces Poking a Dead Frog.

Why it wins:

- It's a method, not stories about writers. Kalan's argument is that jokes come from a repeatable process, not inspiration. He lays out a sequence: find what's absurd from the teller's point of view, say the point plainly, pick a premise, fit it to a known joke shape, twist it, set the tone, then trim the wording until it's short, clear and specific. Then one chapter each on structure, premise, voice, tone, wording and audience. An agent can run that sequence on every line.
- Later chapters apply it to narrative comedy and visual humor, which matter for animated shorts.
- Kalan was head writer of The Daily Show with Jon Stewart, where jokes are written and rewritten on deadline every day, and of the Netflix Mystery Science Theater 3000 revival, which is dense jokes over picture. Jon Stewart, Kristen Schaal and John Hodgman endorse it, and 99% Invisible built an episode around it.
- Its critics say it's a bit academic, with more definitions than examples in the last third. The front two-thirds is the part the agent needs.

**Why Poking a Dead Frog drops:** it's great reading about how comedy writers think, but it's interviews. Slate's review said plainly that it doesn't teach you how to be funny. Keep it in mind as enjoyable reading, not as a teaching book.

**Would have been a co-pick, but no ebook:** **Comedy Writing for Late-Night TV** (Joe Toplyn, 2014). The most step-by-step joke book there is, by a former Letterman head writer; its method was precise enough to become a patented joke-writing program. Paperback only: there's nothing on Kindle, Apple Books or Google Play.

**Runners-up:**

- **The Elements of Humor** (Scott Dikkers, December 2024). The Onion's founder lists the kinds of humor (exaggeration, wordplay, shock, reference and so on) with exercises: a good punch-up checklist, though it's aimed as much at speakers as writers. Apple Books $14.99, Google Play $14.
- **The Comic Toolbox** (Vorhaus), from area 2.

**A gap no book fills:** none of these teaches the writers'-room joke pass directly: runners, callbacks, alternate lines, the layered density of Arrested Development or Community. Kalan's chapter on narrative comedy comes closest. The agent will have to learn that layer from the shows themselves on the shelf.

**Ebook:** Kindle $4.99, Apple Books $4.99, Google Play $4.99 on sale ($9.99 list), ebooks.com $4.99 (copy-protected EPUB). The sale price may end.

## 5. Writing ads, for launch-film voiceovers

**The pick: Hey Whipple, Squeeze This, sixth edition** (Luke Sullivan, Wiley, February 2022). The "no ebook" claim was wrong: Wiley sells the ebook itself.

Why it wins:

- It covers all three parts of the job in one book: finding the idea, writing it short, and making a TV or video spot people don't skip. It has chapters on brevity and simplicity, on edgy and funny work, and on brand stories.
- It's written in a funny voice, which helps an agent that has to write comedy.
- Working creatives recommend it more than any other ad book: a former creative director ranks it near the top of his list, an executive creative director calls it the easiest way in, and it's assigned in university advertising courses.
- The sixth edition is nearly all new: it adds social video, brand storytelling and making things spread. No reason to buy an older one when this one costs $18.
- Its weak spot: one reader says it gives a way of seeing more than a step-by-step method. Barry's book, below, is the step-by-step one.

**Runners-up:**

- **The Advertising Concept Book, third edition** (Pete Barry, 2016). The most structured book on finding ad ideas, with a chapter on TV scripts; the most common required text in university ad courses. Older, and built around print ads. Its ebook is a page-picture file that readers say you can't highlight. Apple Books $16.99, Google Play $29.73.
- **Read Me: 10 Lessons for Writing Great Copy** (Roger Horberry and Gyles Lingwood, 2014). The best book on writing copy sentence by sentence, with exercises, but little on video scripts. Apple Books and Google Play $15.99.
- **Predatory Thinking** (Dave Trott, 2013). Sharp on ideas and wit, but short stories rather than method. Apple Books and Google Play $12.99.

**Ebook:** Kindle $18.00 (flowing text), Google Play $18.00, Wiley direct $18.00. Not on Apple Books.

## 6. Writing for animation (borrowed, not replaced)

**Animated Storytelling** (Liz Blazer, second edition, 2019) is already in Motioncraft's book folder as an ebook. It covers story for short animated films and the planning around them well.

**The gap it leaves:** how to build and escalate a visual gag, comic dialogue, and the page format of an animated comedy script. Kaplan, Kalan and the UCB manual cover most of the comedy side between them, so nothing needs buying now.

**If the gap ever shows on a real job:** **Animation Writing and Development** (Jean Ann Wright, 2005) has chapters on comedy and gag writing, dialogue and animation script format, and an animation news site's editor called it one of the best writing books he'd read. It's old and pricey: Google Play $43.11, Apple Books $54.99. A cheaper, thinner option is **How to Write for Animation** (Jeffrey Scott, 2003), by a writer with three Emmys and hundreds of TV scripts: Apple Books $9.99.
