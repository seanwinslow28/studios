# Systemcraft's expertise

One brief per area of the craft: what a master knows that a beginner doesn't. The lead reads a brief when a job reaches that area, or hands it to a helper working there.

A good brief:

- Names the area in a few words and says when to read it.
- Teaches the fundamentals as working knowledge: what good looks like, the common ways it goes wrong, and how a master fixes each one.
- Gives examples, ideally from real work Sean has seen or made.
- Stays craft, not procedure. No steps to follow, no sign-offs, no rules about other rules.
- Fits in about 1,500 words. When it grows past that, split it.

## The briefs

In the order a design usually meets them:

- **Choosing the shape.** One prompt, a fixed workflow, an agent, or several. Read at the start of any design.
- **What the model sees and remembers.** What goes in front of the model, in what order, and how it carries anything from one run to the next.
- **The tools it may use, and its permissions.** The kit, what it can reach, and the brakes that sit outside the model.
- **Where a person steps in.** Setting it up, letting it run, handling what it can't, handing work back, and how much to trust it.
- **How a design is judged.** Reading real runs, counting failures, and checks that agree with a person.
- **What it costs to run.** Money, waiting time and a person's attention, with a range and a ceiling.
- **Failing safely.** Small failures, alarms that reach someone, and every path ending at a person or a safe stop.
- **Auditing a system that is already running.** Starting from what it did, not what it was meant to do.

Each brief closes with "What a strong system designer would notice": a short list of things a master spots at once. The lead uses that section for the teaching part of a review page, since Sean can't be expected to judge a system design by eye.

The books are where the fundamentals come from, not the ceiling. A brief is written in the studio's own words and never copies book text.
