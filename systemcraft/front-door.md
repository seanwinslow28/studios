# Systemcraft's front door

What this studio's front door adds to the shared front-door guide in craftwork's guides folder. The shared guide sets the method; this page says only what designs for AI systems need on top.

**Where the job lives.** In a folder named `<job>-<date>`, in one of two places named in `systemcraft/folders.local`, which git ignores. If that file is missing, ask Sean for the paths; if a folder it names isn't there yet, make it.

- **His own systems** go in his Systemcraft jobs folder beside the studios repo.
- **A system for his day job** goes in the Systemcraft folder of his private work repo. Nothing from it lands in this repo, in a personal job's folder, or anywhere public.

Ask which it is before anything else, because it decides where every file goes.

**A new design, a change, or an audit.** Ask next which of three jobs this is, because each starts in a different place:

- **A new design:** nothing runs yet. The job ends in a design Sean can rule on and, where it's worth proving, a cheap sketch that runs.
- **A change to something running:** read what it does today first, in its files and its real runs, then design the change.
- **An audit of something running:** start from what it did, not what it was meant to do, as `systemcraft/expertise/auditing-a-running-system.md` says.

**From another studio.** When Sean brings a brief from Productcraft, read it as a head start: its answers become your recommendations below, and what he locked there stays locked. Whether the idea is worth building was settled there; don't reopen it.

**The brainstorm.** Most asks here want quick: Sean usually knows what the system is for, and the question is how it should work. Offer wide when the seed is open ("something that helps me write") and nobody knows yet what kind of system it is. The textbook answers are the set-ups anybody would reach for first, from one well-written prompt to a team of agents; if one of them is it, stop there.

## What the ask must pin down

In this order, one question each, until every answer is a named specific:

1. **What it's for, and what "it worked" looks like.** The work the system does for a person, counted: "a morning note in my inbox by 9 am with last night's three best ideas", not "a smarter assistant". For an audit, the one question Sean wants answered.
2. **Where Sean is in it.** Whether he makes things with it at the keyboard, checks on it now and then, or it runs while he sleeps. How much of his time it may take, and who hears when it breaks.
3. **What's already there.** The skills, scripts, notes, past runs and transcripts it builds on. Look in the files before asking; ask only for what they can't tell you. For an audit, where it runs and how to read it.
4. **What it touches.** The private material it reads (his notes, day-job work), any outside text it takes in (web pages, emails, other people's files), and anything it can send, post or change. Name all three plainly; the design is judged on them.
5. **The limits.** Money a run and a month, whether it stays on his Claude plan, which Mac it runs on, the deadline, and any tool or route Sean named, which becomes a must-have.
6. **What this is not.** The bigger system it would be tempting to design, the build itself, and the nearest wrong versions: more agents than the job needs, a dashboard where a person needed telling, a demo that's never used.

## What good looks like here

- **The shelf first:** `systemcraft/research/`. The long-job harness (a maker and a separate checker with written pass marks, around the model) is Sean's pick, and his own audit of his overnight agents is the studio's best work.
- **Then where systems are shown with their results:** the engineering write-ups of the model makers and the companies that published numbers, honest post-mortems, and the practitioners who publish their failure counts and how they judged their systems.
- **Measure, don't admire:** the work it got done for a person, counted; why it isn't simpler; where a person steps in and what reaches them; what a run costs; how it was judged and its failure counts; and what broke, and how it was found.

## What the front door leaves to the job

- **No set-up picked.** One prompt, a fixed workflow, an agent or several is the first lock in step 3.
- **No prompts, tool list or folder layout.** Those are drawn in the rough design.
- **No cost estimate** beyond Sean's ceiling. The range is worked out from today's prices in the job.
- **No build.** Systemcraft designs and sketches; a design that's going to be built reaches Devcraft only through Sean, at its front door.
