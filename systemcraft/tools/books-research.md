## What Sean decided

On November 16, 2026 Sean bought Evals for AI Engineers and Designing Agentive Technology, both with no copy lock. He did not buy Thinking in Systems. He asked whether Meadows's free essay would do instead. The lead said yes, for the reasons below, and Sean agreed the same day: "Yes, the essay."

- The essay explains the basics itself (what builds up and drains away in a system, the loops that steady it or run away with it, delays) and then ranks twelve places to step in. That ranking is the part Systemcraft uses most: it is how to decide where a check or a person goes.
- What only the book adds is a chapter on eight common ways systems go wrong. The two that matter most for AI systems, chasing the wrong number and gaming the rules, are already on the shelf as real cases.
- Every ebook of it is copy-protected, so it is also the hardest of the six for the lead to turn into notes.

The essay is free to read but all rights are reserved, so the studio keeps notes in the lead's own words, never the essay's text. Look at the book again if a real job shows the essay isn't enough.

## 1. Telling whether an AI system works

**The pick: Evals for AI Engineers** (Shreya Shankar and Hamel Husain; O'Reilly, first edition, October 2026; 240 pages).

- It is the full method behind the field guide on the shelf: read real runs, count the failures, build checks that agree with a person, keep checking once it's live.
- Twelve chapters: the foundations, finding failures, measuring (automated graders, long chats, retrieval, tools and agents), and running it in production.
- **Its weak spot:** too new for outside reviews. The case for it rests on the authors: their course on the same material shows 4.7 out of 5 from over 1,000 reviews.
- **The catch:** Amazon lists a different book with almost the same title, from August 2026 by another author. It isn't this one.

**Ebook:** $67.99 at ebooks.com, as PDF and EPUB marked free of copy locks. Google Play has it at the same price and also says it has no lock. Kobo may have it for about $49; that page would not open for the scout, so it's worth a look by hand.

**Free first:** the authors' own question-and-answer guide, "AI Evals: Everything You Need to Know" (https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/).

## 2. How systems behave over time

**The pick: Thinking in Systems** (Donella Meadows; Chelsea Green, 2008; 240 pages).

- No AI book teaches this as well: loops that steady a system and loops that run away, delays, and the few places where a small change does the most. That last part is how to decide where a check or a person goes.
- 4.6 from about 6,500 Amazon ratings, eighteen years on.
- **The catch:** every ebook found is copy-protected ($16.99 at ebooks.com, Google Play and Kindle). The publisher sells only the paperback, $22.95. A protected ebook can still become notes by photographing its pages, as Scriptcraft did for one of its books; it is more work.

**Runner-up, with no lock:** An Introduction to General Systems Thinking (Gerald Weinberg, 1975), $9.99 on Leanpub. More abstract, fewer tools.

**Free first:** Meadows's essay "Leverage Points: Places to Intervene in a System", the book's best chapter in early form (https://donellameadows.org/archives/leverage-points-places-to-intervene-in-a-system/).

## 3. Failing safely

**The pick: Release It!**, second edition (Michael Nygard; Pragmatic Bookshelf, 2018; 376 pages). Devcraft already has it, so there is nothing to buy.

- How running systems fail, and the patterns that contain the damage: time limits, cut-offs that trip when something keeps failing, walls between parts, failing fast. Each applies to a workflow that calls a model or a tool that can hang, loop or return rubbish.

**Free extras:** Richard Cook's "How Complex Systems Fail" (https://how.complexsystems.fail/), eighteen short points; and Google's two reliability books, free at https://sre.google/books/. Devcraft's copy of Observability Engineering is also worth borrowing, for what to record about a running system.

## 4. Where a person steps in

**The pick: Designing Agentive Technology** (Christopher Noessel; Rosenfeld Media, 2017; 280 pages).

- How to design something that acts for a person while they aren't watching: setting it up, letting it run, handling the exceptions, handing work over and taking it back.
- **Its weak spot:** written before today's models, so its examples are narrow, single-purpose AI. The questions it asks are still the right ones.

**Ebook:** $33.99 direct from rosenfeldmedia.com, which says its ebooks have no copy lock. The same price at ebooks.com buys a locked copy, so buy it from the publisher.

**Free extras:** Google's People + AI Guidebook (https://pair.withgoogle.com/guidebook/) and Microsoft's 18 guidelines for people working with AI.

## 5. Designing agents: no book yet

No agent book from 2025 or 2026 is a standard yet. The two best candidates:

- **AI Agents: The Definitive Guide** (Nicole Koenigstein; O'Reilly, 2026; 378 pages). Its chapters match this studio's work almost line for line: shapes, tools and permissions, safe running, judging, memory, cost. But it has one rating and no outside review. $67.99 at ebooks.com with no lock.
- **Build an AI Agent (From Scratch)** (Hur and Song; Manning, July 2026; 336 pages). Builds an agent in plain code, starting from the difference between a fixed workflow and a loop the model steers. 4.8 from 39 ratings. $28.79 on sale at manning.com, with no lock.

**Read instead, free:** Anthropic's engineering posts, several of which are on the shelf (https://www.anthropic.com/engineering), starting with "Building effective agents".

## 6. Security: no book

This area moves faster than books do. Simon Willison's running series on prompt injection (https://simonwillison.net/tags/prompt-injection/) and the two OWASP top-ten lists are better and current.
